  • Blue is now filled in for 335 more languages, and the map shows how each one splits the colour. 261 have a word of their own for blue. 52 use one word for blue and green, as Tzeltal yax, Tamazight azegzaw, Chichewa wobiriwira, Okinawan ōsan and Toki Pona laso do. 22 borrowed theirs, usually from Spanish azul or English blue.
  • Orange is filled in for 90 more languages, coloured by where the word came from. 28 follow Persian nārang (as in naranja), 9 say portakal after Portugal, and 18 call it the “China” fruit (Dutch sinaasappel). 35 use a local word, for example 橙 in Jin and Northern Min.
2026-10-02 — Tea, coffee and sugar in 856 more places ☕
  • The three words that colour the map by how they travelled, filled in for hundreds of languages that lacked them. Tea split into the overland cha and the sea-borne te; coffee almost everywhere goes back to Arabic qahwa; sugar comes from Sanskrit śarkarā, or from Arabic as-sukkar through Spanish and Portuguese, or is Chinese 糖. The Algonquian languages of North America brew a “leaf drink” instead.
  • Moroccan Arabic tea is now atay, the word people actually say, which came by sea, not the Standard Arabic shāy.

2026-10-02 — The Han Map grows to 95 characters 🀄
  • Eighteen new characters, chosen because they change the most from place to place: 我 大 白 茶 飛 無 兒 黃 家 生 不 青 紅 黑 知 雨 石 死. “I” starts with ŋ- in Cantonese, Wu and Hakka, with ɡ- in Hokkien, and with nothing at all in Mandarin. 飛 keeps an old p- in Min and Hakka. 黃 is n̂g in Hokkien and vàng in Vietnamese. 茶 splits the world’s two words for tea.
  • Then eight more: 你 小 多 個 熱 鼻, and 長 in its two senses, “long” (cháng) and “grow / elder” (zhǎng). 你 shows the Hokkien and Wu habit of saying “you” with another word, 汝 or 爾. 多 shows the Min word 濟 for “many”.
  • And eight more: 遠 老 冷 香 年 女 飯 雪. 飯 keeps an old p- in Min (Hokkien pn̄g, Fuzhou puoŋ) where everyone else has f-. 雪 shows the three fates of the old final stop: -t, a glottal catch, or nothing. 年, 女 and 冷 show Korean North and South differ in how they treat an initial n- or l-.
  • Every reading comes from that place’s own survey or dictionary. Where a place has none, its cell is left empty rather than borrowed from a neighbour.
  • Nine more rows rebuilt from their own surveys. The new characters exposed nine rows whose older readings were copied from somewhere else: Dongguan, Nanning and Zhongshan Cantonese were really Guangzhou, and Hangzhou, Ningbo, Jiaxing, Shexian Hui, Pinghua and Nanchang had tones that matched no survey. All 79 characters now come from each town’s own survey. Dongguan turns out to merge -m/-n into -ŋ (三 ʃaŋ), and Jiaxing splits one tone in two.
  • Two new groups in the character picker, “Colors & qualities” and “People & life”. All eight group names now appear in every language the site speaks; before, Korean, Chinese and other readers saw them in Japanese.
