2026-10-06 — Time Map: 16 more living calendars, sorted by how they are used 🗓️
  • Calendars in use are now grouped as official (Japan’s eras, Nepal, Iran, Ethiopia…), popular but not official (the Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese lunar calendars, Java, Bali, Kurdistan, the Akan of Ghana, Iceland), and religious or community calendars (Bahá’í, Parsi, Dawoodi Bohra, the K’iche’ Maya day count, the Coptic and Orthodox churches). A Modern / Historical switch, as on the Word Map, separates them from the calendars that stopped counting.
  • New: Nepal’s Vikram Samvat (from the official month tables), the Vietnamese âm lịch on Vietnam’s own time zone, the Javanese calendar with its five-day market week, Bali’s 210-day Pawukon, the Kurdish, Akan, Old Icelandic, Bahá’í, Parsi and Dawoodi Bohra calendars, and the 260-day count Maya daykeepers still keep in Guatemala. Each was checked against real dates, such as Galungan, Akwasidae and 1 Suro 1960 on 17 June 2026.
  • Also new: the Tibetan, Mongolian and Bhutanese calendars (the Tibetan way of skipping and doubling days, after Svante Janson’s mathematics, checked against his tables for 2000–2030), the Myanmar calendar, which Myanmar’s government documents carry beside the Gregorian, and Cambodia’s Khmer lunar calendar — each in its own script and numerals.
  • Corrections: the Chinese lunar calendar was a day off in some years (the 2027 Spring Festival is on 6 February, not the 7th) and is now taken from an accurate month table. North Korea stopped using the Juche year in October 2024, so it has moved to Historical. Afghanistan switched its official calendar to the lunar Hijri in 2022.

2026-10-06 — Central and Southern Vietnamese pronunciation made consistent 🇻🇳
  • The Huế and Saigon rows followed different rules on the Word Map and the Han Map. The Han Map’s Southern row still ended 鐵 thiết in -t, while the Word Map already wrote Southern đất with -k. The two maps also gave Central Vietnamese different tone values. 255 pronunciations now follow one set of rules per dialect.
  • The rules: tones from Vũ Thanh Phương’s acoustic study of all three regions (1981). Final -n/-t become -ŋ/-k after back vowels in both dialects, so thiết is [tʰiək] and thiên [tʰiəŋ]. Saigon also merges in/it and ên, and has v → [j] and r → [ɹ]; Huế has r → [ʐ]. Southern readings such as Nhứt for 一 stay as they are.
2026-10-06 — Han Map adds 鐵 “iron”, the 105th character ⚒️

    • Mandarin tiě, Cantonese tit3, Hokkien thih / thiat, Shanghainese thiq.
    • Japanese テツ (Kan-on) and テチ (Go-on), Korean 철 and Vietnamese thiết.

2026-10-04 — Middle Korean and Goryeo Korean no longer overlap 🇰🇷

2026-10-02 — The Han Map grows to 95 characters 🀄
  • Eighteen new characters, chosen because they change the most from place to place: 我 大 白 茶 飛 無 兒 黃 家 生 不 青 紅 黑 知 雨 石 死. “I” starts with ŋ- in Cantonese, Wu and Hakka, with ɡ- in Hokkien, and with nothing at all in Mandarin. 飛 keeps an old p- in Min and Hakka. 黃 is n̂g in Hokkien and vàng in Vietnamese. 茶 splits the world’s two words for tea.
