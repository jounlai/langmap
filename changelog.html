2026-07-30 — Strict scholarly review of the reconstructed proto-languages
  • A very strict audit went back over the 21 reconstructed proto-languages and corrected their entries against mainstream comparative-linguistics scholarship. Every proto had a reconstructed word (usually 'water') sitting in the wrong field where its name should be — now fixed. Several homelands and map pins were nudged to the accepted position (Proto-Bantu to the Cameroon Grassfields; Proto-Kra-Dai onto the coast it describes), and several reconstruction dates were corrected or widened to reflect that they are genuinely contested — most notably Proto-Hmong-Mien, whose date had been read as 2500 BCE when the literature places it around 2,500 years ago (roughly 500 BCE). The write-ups were also fixed in all 19 interface languages: e.g. the “schwa indogermanicum” is no longer misattributed to Schleicher, Proto-Ryukyuan now lists the Amami branch, and the disputed Japonic-Koreanic macro-family is presented as the contested hypothesis it is.
2026-07-30 — “My languages”: plot what you speak and share it
  • A new My languages entry in the Word Map's Play menu lets you pick the languages you speak — each with a CEFR level from A1 to C2 (or Native) — and see, live, how many people they reach worldwide, across how many countries and language families. Your picks can be lit up on the map, and a one-tap Create image builds a shareable card: a world map with your languages glowing on it, your total reach and its share of the world's population, and your language list. The reach figure is a cumulative total of each language's speakers (second-language speakers included), so it is an upper bound where big lingua-francas overlap.
2026-07-30 — Review pass on five recently-added words
  • An adversarial review checked the five most-recently-added core words — star, I, you, name and two — across all 1,138 languages, and corrected eleven entries. Two Swiss Standard German cells had slipped into the Alemannic dialect spelling (zwöi → zwei, Stärn → Stern); Squamish, Nuxalk, Guugu Yimithirr and Huli each had a neighbouring word standing in for the target (Squamish 'two' is án̓us, not the word for 'three'); Tundra and Kolyma Yukaghir had their word for 'two' swapped; and a few pronunciations were tidied (Swahili stress, Thulung 'two' नि). Every fix was verified against the descriptive literature before being applied.
2026-07-26 — Reconstructed proto-languages on the historical map
  • The historical map now carries the reconstructed common ancestors of the world's major language families. Proto-Indo-European was already there; it is now joined by seventeen more — Proto-Austronesian, ‑Semitic, ‑Uralic, ‑Dravidian, ‑Bantu, ‑Sino-Tibetan, ‑Afroasiatic, ‑Kartvelian, ‑Mayan, ‑Uto-Aztecan, ‑Austroasiatic, ‑Kra-Dai, ‑Hmong-Mien, ‑Turkic, ‑Mongolic, ‑Tungusic, ‑Japonic and ‑Koreanic (the last two are the single-family ancestors of Japanese and Korean, kept separate from the disputed Proto-Japonic-Koreanic macro; Proto-Ryukyuan was already present). That brings the reconstructed proto-languages to twenty-one, and the Word Map to 1,138 languages and varieties in all. Each shows its reconstructed core-vocabulary forms (marked with a *), a hypothesised homeland and date, and a write-up in every interface language; words that cannot be securely reconstructed are left blank rather than invented, so the sparser, more contested families (e.g. Hmong-Mien) show fewer forms than the well-studied ones (Turkic, Mongolic, Kartvelian, Bantu, Mayan).
  • An adversarial review then re-checked every reconstruction against the family-by-family comparative literature and corrected five forms where a daughter-branch reflex had crept in for the true proto — Proto-Turkic 'eye' is *köz (not the Oghuz *göz), Proto-Mayan 'name' is *bʼih (not the Lowland *kʼaabʼaʔ) and 'drink' *ʔukʼ, Proto-Bantu 'two' *-bɪ̀dɪ̀, and Proto-Kartvelian 'eye' *tʼwal-.
2026-07-26 — Filter the historical map by time period
  • In historical mode, the filter panel gains a single dual-handle time slider running from deep prehistory to the 21st century. Drag the two ends — or grab the coloured bar in the middle to slide the whole window — to show only the languages that existed within that span, so you can see which written and reconstructed languages coexisted around, say, the 15th century BCE. The proto-languages, which carry no living speakers, are also given priority when historical labels are decluttered, so they surface instead of being crowded out.
  • The chosen period now travels in the page URL, so a time-filtered view survives a reload and can be shared. Separately, the Proto-language option in the family filter was corrected to list exactly the reconstructed protos — it no longer sweeps in attested ancient languages such as Vedic Sanskrit or Old Chinese.
2026-07-26 — Family names, localized in more languages
  • The family tree and the family filter were still showing many branch names in English even when the interface was set to another language. Individual language names were already fully localized; now the most visible family and sub-branch labels — Ryukyuan, Tibetic, Qiangic, Cariban, Otomanguean, Indo-Iranian, Finno-Ugric and dozens more — read in Korean and the other interface languages too. The family tree also now loads the same shared translation layer the map already used (so e.g. Germanic and Romance are no longer left in English there), and its data caches were refreshed so it picks up the new proto-languages.
2026-07-21 — Much shorter share links
  • The link in the address bar used to spell out every setting even when it was at its default — #w=water&z=5&lat=25.00&lng=20.00&nat=0&hist=0&pin=0&fs=140&ui=en&g3d=0. Now it only carries what you've actually changed, so a plain share collapses to just #w=water. Move the map, flip a toggle or open a language and those pieces come back — nothing is lost, and every old long link still opens exactly as before. Applies to both the Word Map and the Han Map.
  • When the map has been moved, the three location pieces are now packed into one — p=35.68,139.69,6 (latitude, longitude, zoom) instead of z=6&lat=35.68&lng=139.69 — trimming the link a little further.
  • Fixed along the way: a shared link carrying a language filter could drop the filter the instant it opened (the : in f=family:Romance came back percent-encoded as %3A and wasn't recognised, so the map rewrote the link without it). Filters in shared links now restore reliably, and the filter piece is written in its shorter, un-encoded form.
2026-07-21 — Consistent stress marks across the English accents
  • The primary-stress mark (ˈ) had been applied unevenly: "father" showed as ˈfɑːðə in standard and American English but as plain fɑːðə in the Yorkshire, Scottish, Welsh, Cockney, Australian and other entries. Every multi-syllable single word across the English varieties now carries its stress mark (ˈfɑːðə, ˈwɔːtə, ˈmʌðə, həˈləʊ), so the same word reads the same way from one accent to the next. Single-syllable words (cat, sun) correctly carry none. This pass covered the English varieties; the same tidy-up for other languages can follow.
2026-07-23 — Metadata that reads naturally in every language
  • In the language detail panel, the Countries, Official, Script and Family rows were being translated word-by-word, which left ugly half-English seams in non-English interfaces — Japanese showed "アルジェリア（Kabylie）; ディアスポラ in フランス" for Kabyle. Those four fields now read as proper sentences: "アルジェリア（カビリー）／フランスに離散コミュニティ", "アルジェリア（タマジクト語は2002年以降国語、2016年以降公用語）". Around 630 of the most tangled country/official phrases were fully translated into all 18 interface languages, and a batch of family, branch and script terms (including ones that were simply missing, like Cyrillic) were added.
  • Language-family wording is now consistent too: a family, a branch and a looser grouping each get their own standard suffix in each language (Japanese 語族 / 語派 / 諸語, and the equivalents elsewhere) — so "Niger-Congo (Mande, …, Vai-Kono)" reads "ニジェール・コンゴ語族（マンデ語派、…、ヴァイ・コノ語群）" instead of a mix.
2026-07-23 — Broken and missing description translations, repaired
  • A second, structural sweep of the language write-ups (separate from the factual one) caught a different family of defects: 165 entries were repaired across all nineteen languages. Over a hundred had an English source that literally stopped mid-word — sometimes with an internal "[Provisional…]" work-note that had leaked into the live text and every translation. Seven had no translations at all (the six new British accents and Eastern Cham). The English was rebuilt from authoritative sources where broken, then every language was re-synced to it, so the required languages are now complete for all 1,120 entries. A new automated check (missing / untranslated / English-left-in / cut-off source) guards against regressions on every build.
2026-07-23 — A sweep for descriptions that were confidently wrong
  • The Vai fix turned out not to be a one-off, so every language description was audited for the same failure — an absolute claim ("the only", "the oldest", "the largest", "the first") that isn't actually true. Of the 1,120 descriptions, 387 make such a claim; most are correct, but 67 were not and have been rewritten in English and every translation. A sample: Kannada does not have the most Jnanpith Awards of any Indian language (Hindi does); Silesian was not recognised as a regional language of Poland (the bill was vetoed); Chile's "Real Academia de la Lengua Chilena" doesn't exist (it's the Academia Chilena de la Lengua); Hawaiian is grammatically accusative, not ergative; Sequoyah's Cherokee was not the first indigenous American script (the Maya had one two millennia earlier); Finnish and Swedish are co-equal national languages of Finland, not a language and its "secondary"; and Tongan's war dance is not the (Māori) haka. Each correction was researched, then carried through all nineteen-plus translations so no language keeps the old error.
2026-07-23 — Vai: a description that was confidently wrong
  • The write-up for Vai (the Liberian language with its own remarkable syllabary) called the script "the only" indigenous African writing system invented before colonisation and still in use, and "the oldest" — both untrue. Geʽez and Tifinagh are far older and still very much in use, and Liberia was a settlement of freed African Americans rather than a European colony, so "pre-colonial" was the wrong frame. The corrected version places Vai as one of the earliest and most successful of the roughly two dozen indigenous West African scripts of the 1800s–1900s, notes the scholarly view that exposure to Latin, Arabic and perhaps Cherokee sparked the idea of writing (the signs themselves being largely original), and fixes a "Mande-Mande" typo to Mande–Atlantic. All nineteen translations were rewritten from scratch as natural prose — the Japanese, especially, had read like a machine.
2026-07-21 — British English, subdivided into six more accents
  • Alongside the Cockney, Yorkshire, Scottish and Welsh entries already on the map, six of Britain's most distinctive regional accents join it: Scouse (Liverpool), Geordie (Newcastle), Brummie (Birmingham), Mancunian (Manchester), West Country (Bristol and the South West) and Estuary (the South East). The base "English" entry already carries Received Pronunciation, so RP is not duplicated.
  • The spelling usually stays standard; what changes is the sound. Each of the 150 transcriptions was written by an accent specialist and independently checked for the features that actually define these accents: the northern ones (Scouse, Geordie, Brummie, Mancunian) have no FOOT–STRUT split, so "love" and "sun" rhyme with "put"; Scouse softens its consonants ("back" toward [bax]); Geordie says "house" almost as "hoose"; the West Country is rhotic (the /r/ in "farmer" is sounded) and even keeps a trace of the old "Zummerzet" voicing in "father" [ˈvɑːðɚ]; Estuary swallows its t's ("water" → [ˈwɔːʔə]). Where an accent simply uses a different word — Scouse, Brummie and Mancunian all prefer "ta" for "thanks" — that word is shown instead.
  • Also fixed: Welsh English "thanks" carried a leftover /juː/ in its transcription (the tail of "thank you") that didn't match the word shown; it now reads /θaŋks/.
2026-07-21 — The Amazigh languages, now in Tifinagh
  • Four Berber (Amazigh) languages were written in the Latin alphabet even though their entries advertised Tifinagh — the angular, ancient-looking Libyco-Berber script. They now appear in it: Central Atlas Tamazight, Tashelhit and Tarifit in Neo-Tifinagh (the standard IRCAM form official in Morocco), and Tuareg (Tamasheq) in the traditional Saharan Tifinagh — ⴰⵎⴰⵏ "water", ⵜⴰⴼⵓⴽⵜ "sun", ⴰⵣⵓⵍ "hello". Kabyle deliberately stays in Latin: that is genuinely how Kabyle is written day to day in Algeria, and pretending otherwise would be less accurate, not more.
  • Every spelling was transliterated from the word's own pronunciation and then checked letter-by-letter against the Unicode Tifinagh chart — following the real orthographic rules (the neutral vowel goes unwritten, long consonants are doubled), not a naive letter swap. Tifinagh ships as a small self-hosted webfont so it renders everywhere.
2026-07-21 — Two Cham languages, each in its own script
  • Cham was a single entry written in the Latin alphabet. It is really two languages that split when the kingdom of Champa fell, and each kept a different script. The map now shows both. Western Cham (the Muslim communities of Cambodia and An Giang) is written in Jawi, the Arabic-based orthography they use — ايا, سلام, تريما كاسيه. Eastern Cham, a new entry for the ~100,000 speakers of Ninh Thuận and Bình Thuận in Vietnam, is written in the ancestral Cham script (Akhar Thrah, a Brahmic script descended from the Pallava letters of southern India): ꨀꨳꨩ, ꨦꨤꩌ, ꨟꨢꨥ.
  • Every one of the 25 core words was researched from a scholarly Eastern-Cham dictionary and then independently checked letter-by-letter against the Unicode Cham chart — two words even resolved to spellings attested on Wiktionary. The two languages genuinely differ in vocabulary, not just script: Eastern Cham "star" is bituk, not Western patìk; "thank you" is the native ndua phuel, not the Malay loan the western dialect borrowed. The Cham script is delivered as a tiny self-hosted webfont so it renders the same everywhere.
  • Full descriptions for Eastern Cham are written in English, Japanese, Korean and Chinese, and both names are translated into all nineteen interface languages.
2026-07-21 — Dead languages no longer sit among the living
  • Mochica (Yunga), the language of Peru's north coast that died out in the early 20th century, was appearing on the modern map even though it has no speakers left and carries a historical period. It — and three others in the same situation (Yugh, Babuza, Kulon-Pazeh) — now live on the historical map where they belong. Languages that are merely endangered but still spoken (Manchu, Ubykh) stay put.
  • To make sure this doesn't drift back, the rule is now enforced automatically: any language with zero living speakers and a recorded period must be classified as historical, checked on every build.
2026-07-21 — Gondi settles on one script
  • Gondi cells had been showing two scripts side by side (Devanagari / Telugu), and five words only one — inconsistent, and against the one-word-per-cell rule everywhere else. All 25 now use Devanagari, the script of the larger Gondi-speaking regions (Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra).
2026-07-21 — Southern Vietnamese: the retroflex "s"
  • In the southern dialect the letter s is pronounced with the tongue curled back — /ʂ/ — and kept distinct from x (/s/), unlike the north where the two merge. The word "star" (sao) was still showing the northern /s/; it now reads /ʂaːw/, matching how the rest of the southern column already treats the sound.
2026-07-19 — Historical languages now say when they were spoken
  • In the historical era view every language label carries a second line: the century span it was actually a living, spoken language — Latin 7cBCE–6cCE, Sumerian 31–21cBCE, Old English 5–12c, Gothic 3–8c, Old Khmer 7–15c. It reads in red under the language name.
  • This needed a field the atlas simply didn't have. The dates were buried in prose inside the speaker-count text, and only about half the historical languages had one there at all — the most famous ones (Latin, Ancient Greek, Sumerian, Akkadian, Gothic, Old Norse, Hittite, Coptic, Pali) said nothing but "Extinct". So all 108 spans were researched from scratch and checked by an independent reviewer, in two passes: the second caught that Southeast Asia and the Austronesian world had fallen through a gap in the first, leaving Old Khmer, Old Mon, Old Burmese, Pyu, Old Cham, Chữ Nôm, Old Javanese, Old Malay, Old Tagalog and others blank.
  • Each span is the language's living period, not its afterlife as a scholarly or liturgical language — Latin's line ends in the 6th century, not with the Vatican — and for a historical stage (Old English, Middle Korean) it is that stage's own span. Everything is written one way: centuries, an en dash, BCE only where needed, averaging under seven characters so it fits under the name.
  • The historical view now shows only historical languages. It used to also plot each one's modern descendant as a red reference label, which crowded the map.
  • Also fixed: Old Mon was labelled "Middle Mongolian" in English — a different language on a different continent — while its family, its countries and all nineteen other translations correctly said Old Mon. (The real Middle Mongolian is a separate entry and was untouched.)
2026-07-19 — Word Map: a 30th word, dopamine
  • A modern scientific word joins the map. Dopamine was only isolated in 1957, so unlike sushi or computer it has no ancient analogue at all — the historical languages are simply left blank rather than given a stand-in. Nearly every living language borrowed the international term and bent it to its own sounds and script: ドーパミン, 도파민, дофамин, دوبامين, ντοπαμίνη, डोपामाइन. Chinese is the interesting exception, writing it 多巴胺 — a sound-and-sense rendering where 多巴 carries "dopa" and 胺 means "amine".
  • Three review rounds took it from an initial 35 languages to 107, adding among others the Caucasus and Central Asia (Georgian დოფამინი, Armenian Դոֆամին, Kazakh Дофамин), the Indic and Dravidian scripts (ડોપામાઇન, டோபமைன், ഡോപാമിൻ), Tibetan ཌོ་པ་མིན, Burmese ဒိုပါမင်း, Lao ໂດປາມີນ, Thai โดพามีน and a long tail of smaller European languages (Icelandic dópamín, Irish dopaimín, Welsh, Breton, Sorbian…).

2026-07-18 — Two languages that were quietly wrong: Western Yugur and the Chữ Nôm words
  • Western Yugur — a Turkic language of Buddhist herders in Gansu — was showing Uyghur words written in the Uyghur Arabic script. That is the wrong language and the wrong alphabet (Western Yugur is unwritten; scholars romanise it), and the greetings were even Islamic loanwords its speakers don't use. Checked against the standard grammar and the ASJP and Starostin databases, its 24 words are now the genuine Western Yugur forms in Latin: sun kün (not Uyghur quyash), eye kez, two šigɨ, house jü, and so on.
  • Chữ Nôm — the old Vietnamese character script — now carries tones on every word, like the rest of the Vietnamese data, and eight characters that actually spelled a different word were fixed (the one for "dog" had been the character for "rat"). Sushi's historical Akkadian and Old Javanese cells moved into their real scripts — cuneiform 𒅆𒅅𒄣 and Javanese ꦝꦺꦁ — instead of a romanised gloss.
  • Rare scripts now display instead of boxes. Javanese, Baybayin (Tagalog), Brahmi, New Tai Lue and Tai Viet were named in the font list but no device actually ships those fonts, so they rendered as tofu (□□□). They are now self-hosted as tiny embedded subsets, so ꦄꦏꦸ, ᜊᜓᜇᜓ and the rest show properly.
2026-07-18 — Speaker numbers now read naturally in Japanese, Chinese and Korean

Along the way every malformed range was tidied to one house style (Amdo Tibetan's 800K-1.8M → 0.8–1.8M, and 30-odd others), and a checker now guards the format so they stay consistent.

2026-07-17 — Word Map: two new words, computer and sushi
  • Two modern concepts join the map. Sushi is a loanword the world took from Japanese, so it is sushi almost everywhere — but each language says it its own way: Australian sʉːʃiː, Swedish sʉːɧi, Greek σούσι, seseo Spanish /susi/, Cyrillic суши, and the Chinese topolects read 壽司 with their own tones. Computer is split between languages that borrowed it (computer / компьютер / 컴퓨터) and those that built their own (电脑, ordinateur, dator, رایانه, tölva, bilgisayar, கணினி, Tibetan གློག་ཀླད).
  • Both are partial words (marked 🧪): filled where a real, sourced form exists — 294 languages for sushi, 355 for computer — and left blank rather than guessed elsewhere. For the ancient languages, which had neither thing, the map shows the era's nearest analogue. Sushi's ancestor is narezushi — fish fermented in cooked grain — so its historical cells trace that dish to its Mekong-basin origin: Old Khmer ប្រហុក (prahok), Old Thai ปลาร้า (pla ra), Old Tagalog ᜊᜓᜇᜓ (burong isda), Old Burmese ငါးပိ (ngapi); further west, where there was no such dish, salted or dried fish stands in — Old Norse skreið (stockfish), Ottoman lakerda, Ancient Greek τάριχος, Latin salsāmentum. For computer: Latin calculātor (a human reckoner), the ἄβαξ abacus and Japanese 算木 counting rods.
