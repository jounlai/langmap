2026-09-01 — The article pages now show you the thing instead of linking to it 🗺️
  • The reading pages carry little instructions — "compare Taa, Khoekhoe, Xhosa and Zulu", "zoom to the Kalahari" — and until now they were only links. You had to leave the article, load the map, and come back. Now the page just answers them where they stand: a set of languages becomes an actual comparison table, and a place becomes an actual map.
  • The comparison tables use the same facts the language pages do — family, speaker numbers, and the word forms with their pronunciation — so the click-language article now shows you ǃqhàa, ǁgam-i and amanzi side by side rather than telling you to go and look. Where the article names a word, that word is the first column.
  • The maps are drawn from the site's own country outlines, at build time. Fifty-seven locations cover every article. There is no script on the page, nothing is fetched from anywhere, and the languages the article is about are pinned on the map with their names in your language — Pirahã shows up as ピダハン語 on the Japanese page and as пирахан on the Russian one.
  • Both work with no JavaScript and are in the page source, so they are readable by anyone the interactive map does not reach — and a search engine sees the table too.
2026-08-31 — Middle Korean was unreadable on the Word Order map, and one letter had been left out of the atlas entirely ᄆᆞ
  • Old Hangul — the 15th-century letters with the dot vowel ᆞ, ᄆᆞᄅᆞᆯ, ᄒᆞ노라 — needs a font almost nobody has. Google's Korean webfonts leave the whole block out, iPhones have nothing for it, and desktop Chrome puts the letters side by side instead of stacking them into syllables. The Word Map and the Han Map each shipped their own hand-cut font for it. The Word Order map never had one, so every Middle Korean sentence on the front page — 나ᄂᆞᆫ 새로온 ᄆᆞᄅᆞᆯ … — has been broken or blank for its whole life. Reported by the owner.
  • The two fonts that did exist were each one letter short of their own page, because both were cut to exactly the letters the data contained on the day they were made: the Word Map's was missing ᄄ, the Han Map's ᅲ. There is now one font, cut to the whole range instead of to today's data — every conjoining letter, the archaic ㅸ ㅭ ㅿ ㆁ ㆆ, and the 방점 tone dots — shared by every page and fetched only by readers who actually open a Korean historical row.
  • Jeju's word for "daughter" was missing from the atlas because of this. Jeju writes it ᄄᆞᆯ, and the cell had been left deliberately blank with a note saying the letter ᄄ could not be displayed. It is filled in now.
  • A new check reads the shipped font's actual character list, compares it against every Korean historical letter in the data, and confirms each page both declares the font and actually uses it — the three ways this has broken so far.

2026-08-31 — Bear gets colours, and they turn out to be a map of where bears live 🐻
  • "Bear" now colours the map by what each language did with the word, and the pattern is not the one the entry was written around. The story everyone knows is taboo avoidance — Germanic swapped in "the brown one", Slavic the kenning "honey-eater", so that English bear and Russian медведь are both euphemisms for a word people stopped saying. That is real, and it is now purple on the map. But it covers 112 of 587 languages, and it is a belt: Germanic, Slavic, Baltic, Irish, Finnish, Khanty "the guest", Mansi "forest-eater", Yukaghir "big grandfather", Shor "old man". Northern, and hunting cultures.
  • The much bigger colour is green — 438 languages that simply kept their word. And they cluster exactly where you would guess: Iranian and Armenian keep the 6,000-year-old Indo-European word, ten Daghestanian languages keep theirs, one word runs from Old Chinese through Tibetan, Burmese, Zaiwa and Nuosu, another covers every Formosan language, another every Tai language, and Muskogean and Athabaskan each cover a whole family. These are the peoples who live with bears and never stopped naming them.
  • The third colour is a latitude band. Roughly between 15°N and 35°S, outside the Andes, there are no bears — and the languages there have no word. Some borrowed one (blue on the map), a few built a description, and about 200 have nothing at all, which is why those languages are blank rather than filled in. Bible translations say it plainly: Huli, Kalam, Mussau, Enga, Kosraean, Yapese, Motu, Chuukese and six Australian languages all render "bear" with the English word, usually inside a frame — "the animal called bea".
  • So the three colours are very nearly predicted by whether bears live there. That is a sharper claim than "half of Indo-European was afraid of the word", and it only works because the empty rows stayed empty: filling them with oso and urso would erase the pattern the colours exist to show. Four languages are still marked "not yet determined" — Marathi, Bulgarian, Macedonian and Sinhala each use a word whose history is genuinely unsettled.
  • The word went from 343 languages to 587 on the way. One find worth keeping: Muisca, in the Colombian Andes, called the bear guia — which is also its word for "older brother" and "older sister". Renaming the animal as a relative is what Yakut, Dolgan, Nanai and Udege do in Siberia, and here it is again on the other side of the world.

2026-08-31 — The map can now show only the languages you picked 👥
  • A new switch under the pin toggle limits the map to your comparison list. Add four languages, flick it on, and the other eleven hundred go away. It deliberately ignores the zoom rules that normally hide small languages — otherwise turning it on while zoomed out would hide the very languages you asked to see, since a list of Rotokas and Hawaiian is invisible below a certain zoom.
  • The amber ring disappears while it is on. That ring marks "this language is in your comparison" — and when the map contains nothing else, it marks nothing. Four ringed labels on an otherwise empty map is noise, so the ring steps aside and the labels are shown plain. The blue selection ring stays, because only one language can be selected at a time and that still tells you something.
  • The switch is disabled while the list is empty, since "only these" would then mean "none", and a blank map reads as breakage rather than as a filter. Emptying the list while it is on turns it off rather than leaving you with a white screen and no visible cause.
2026-08-31 — A check that had been quietly skipping eight files, and what it was hiding 🔍
  • One of the site's own data checks had stopped reading eight of the sixty-seven word files, and said nothing useful about it. It strips out one kind of code comment before scanning but not the other, and every word file opens with a paragraph of English prose in the second kind. An apostrophe in that paragraph — “the map's first typological word” — looked to the scanner like the start of a quotation that never ended, so it read to the end of the file without ever finding the data. Every file whose opening paragraph had an odd number of apostrophes was affected. It printed a note for each and then reported zero problems, which is the worst possible combination.
  • What it had been missing: fifteen languages listed twice in “hundred”. The file had grown a second Austronesian section and a second Africa section, so Javanese, Sundanese, Cebuano, Malagasy, Māori, Samoan, Tongan, Zulu, Xhosa, Shona and five more each appeared in two places. Nothing wrong was ever displayed, because the later entry wins and in four cases the later entry was the better one — but anyone editing the first copy would have seen no effect at all.
  • The reviewer made the mirror-image mistake the same day. Reading a different check that prints its first five warnings and then a line saying how many more there are, they reported five problems where there were seventy-seven. A truncated output read as a total, twice, in opposite directions. Both tools now say what they mean, and a file the scanner cannot open is counted as a failure rather than mentioned in passing.
  • Fixed alongside: seventy-seven reconstructed forms carrying a stem-boundary mark in the pronunciation as well as the spelling, seventeen cells that looked identical to their neighbours but were stored as different sequences of characters, eight missing spaces that ran two sentences together in the Korean, Vietnamese, Indonesian and Hindi definitions, and the at-sign entry, whose explanation existed in four of the site's twenty-three interface languages while every other word had all twenty-three.
2026-08-30 — 129 Chinese cells were wearing the wrong tone, and the giveaway was 土 🀄
  • Every Chinese dialect gives one pitch to each historical tone class, so a row that disagrees with itself has a mistake in it. A check for that has been running since yesterday, but only over two of the four classes — the other two are written so inconsistently across the map (a Mandarin third tone is spelled ˨˩˦ in one row and ˧˩˧ in the next) that the obvious version of the check is useless. Teaching it that a shorter contour which is a prefix of a longer one is the same claim, and that four particular characters are unreliable witnesses, brought the noise down far enough to read. It found 114 disagreements. 129 cells later there are 34.
  • The single most useful character turned out to be 土, “earth”. In five dialects — Nanning Pinghua, Putian, Jian'ou, Quanzhou and Hengyang — the whole 上聲 class had been filled with one wrong value and 土 was the only correct cell in it. Twice the wrong value is identifiably that dialect's departing tone, which is what a column copied one row too far looks like. Because 土 alone against six is exactly the shape of a cell that is itself wrong, each row was settled from outside it: a published per-city reading for 土 and another for 水, two independent syllables of the same class. They agreed every time.
  • “Dog” and “good” travel as a pair. In six more dialects those two cells, and only those two, carry a tone the rest of the row does not use — Taiwanese, Shanghai, Wuhan, Chengdu, Taiyuan, Tianjin. Two cells moving together is a batch, not a coincidence.
  • “Rain” splits both ways, which is why it had to be looked up rather than assumed. In Mandarin and Cantonese the map's 雨 was wrong and its 五 “five” was right. In Chengdu and in Meixian Hakka it is the other way round: 雨 was right all along and the neighbours were wrong. Guessing from the majority would have made half of these worse.
  • One cell was examined and deliberately left alone. Jianghuai Mandarin writes “tree” as sɿ, which looks exactly like the errors above and is a real feature — around Yangzhou a whole group of words merges into that vowel, and the row's other cells confirm it is that kind of dialect. Flattening a regional feature into the standard shape is the more expensive mistake, and this map has made it before.
  • The remaining 34 need dialect-specific sources rather than a lookup, and they are written down in order: the Wu word for “two”, which reads the same wrong way in Ningbo, Suzhou, Wenzhou and Jinhua alike, and five dialects no reference table covers.
2026-08-30 — Milk reaches 727 languages, and Mingrelian's sun is its milk 🥛
  • Milk went from 496 languages to 727. A third of it came from scripture rather than from any database: 102 translations read at the six passages where milk actually occurs — Hebrews 5, 1 Corinthians 3, Genesis 18, Deuteronomy 32, Exodus 3 and Isaiah 55 — keeping only a word that turns up in three or more of them. That is where nearly all of Papua New Guinea, Australia, Amazonia and Mesoamerica came from: Warlpiri lampurnu, Eastern Arrernte werlatye, Hopi piihu, Enga andu, Chol chuʼ, Kuna nunis.
  • Eight unrelated families build the word from “breast”. Khanty and Mansi say “breast water” outright, and so do Shan and Tai Lue; Hup, Tuyuca, Muisca, Isthmus Nahuatl, Bribri, Twi, Tetum, Fijian, Shuar and Kosraean each derive it from their own word for breast, exactly as Hawaiian waiū does.
  • Mingrelian's word for milk is its word for the sun, and the proof is the language next door. The map refuses any cell that comes out identical to another cell in the same row, because that is almost always a mis-glossed source. This one is real: Georgian keeps მზე “sun” apart from რძე “milk”, both become bʒa in the Zan branch, and Laz — Mingrelian's sister, also on the map — still shows the two halves separately, ბჯა for milk beside მჟორა for sun. Mingrelian let them collide. Accepted, with the Laz argument recorded so nobody removes it again.
  • A third collision was refused, and the difference is the point. A database offered Naxi no³³ for milk, which is also that row's word for “you”. But the same database's Naxi disagrees with the map's Naxi on water, eye and drink — so it is describing a different variety, and its homophony says nothing about this one. Left empty.
  • Deliberate blanks, as always: there is no reconstructable Indo-European noun for the liquid — the handbooks offer a verb and a Greek-and-Latin-only word — so the ancestral row stays empty. Makuxi's only attestation is a compiler's phrase, “cow's breast juice”. And Taishanese 牛奶 turns out to mean condensed milk.
2026-08-29 — Four reaches 980 languages, and Mycenaean still spells it with a q 𐀤𐀵𐀫
  • “Four” went from 330 languages to 980, and “hundred” from 403 to 668. Both came out of the same book: a catalogue of the numeral systems of the world's languages, which has the useful property that it never gives you one number on its own. It gives you one through five as well. So before taking a language's word for four, the map could check that book's one, two, three and five against the four this map already had. Where they matched, the source was describing the same language. Where they did not, it was describing a neighbour, and the offer was refused. About half were refused.
  • Twice the catalogue itself was wrong, and the same check found it. Efik's “hundred” was printed as kíét, which is Efik for one — a slip inside the book. Its two hundred is íkíé íbá, so the hundred is íkíé, and that is what the map now says. Tzotzil's hundred was the Tzeltal word: two Mayan languages spoken next door to each other, and one entry had wandered into the other's row.
  • Mycenaean Greek writes four as 𐀤𐀵𐀫 qe-to-ro. The reconstructed ancestor of the whole family began this word with a kʷ, and Linear B, three hundred years before Homer, still has a syllable-sign for it. By classical Greek it is téssares and the sound is gone. The same row now runs Latin quattuor, Welsh pedwar, Irish ceathair and Mycenaean qe-to-ro side by side, which is the p/q split and its origin in one line. Old Chinese's four is stranger still: *s.li[j]-s, beginning with an l, under a character that is a plain s in every living Chinese dialect.
  • Two languages have no word for four and build one the way they build five. Juǀ'hoan says tsàn ko tsàn, two and two; Kalam says omŋal omŋal. And the hundreds are worth looking at on the map as a band: Navajo's is ten tens, while Yup'ik, Kuna, Tzeltal, K'iche', Purépecha and Ainu all say five twenties — each one visibly containing that language's own word for five. Khoekhoe just says big ten.
  • Refusals recorded rather than quietly skipped: Taa borrows its four from Tswana, Shipibo-Conibo and Pirahã have none, and the catalogue's Kuvi entry is in Odia throughout — a speaker answering in the regional language rather than their own. That last one is the failure mode the whole checking procedure exists to catch.
2026-08-29 — A language in Burkina Faso had been answering in the trade language 🇧🇫

Meixian Hakka gave “love” and “tree” a tone that language uses only for a different kind of syllable. 2026-08-29 — Eleven words the map had left half-finished 🌍
  • Snow, stone, sleep, nose, ear, egg, bird, rain, wind, earth and five all moved at once — some by twenty points, one by forty. Five now reaches 1,012 languages, ear 965, rain 865, stone and nose 861 each. The work had been sitting on disk since an earlier session: someone had pulled these eleven words out of the big comparative databases, saved the results, and never got round to using them. Nobody noticed until the person filling in “egg” went looking for a source and found the file already waiting.
  • The databases turned out to be wrong often enough that checking them was most of the job. They are compiled from field wordlists gathered over a century, and a word sometimes lands in the wrong column. The giveaway is that the proposed word is already sitting in the same language's row under a different heading. Mlabri's “rain” was that language's word for mother. Kuvi's “bird” was its word for fish. Wichí's and Kera's “ear” were their word for tooth; four Tibetan varieties offered their word for nose. And a dozen languages returned, for “rain”, simply their word for water.
  • The commonest mistake of all is offering frost, ice or hail as snow. Hausa's ƙanƙara is ice — the Book of Job sets the two side by side in one line. Sumerian's supposed snow word is glossed “frost” in all five of its attestations. Tigrinya's በረድ covers ice, snow and hail together, and the dictionary that lists it notes that snow there is “very rare and rather unknown”. Zulu and Ndebele have no snow word at all despite the Drakensberg; the word usually offered means frost.
  • Sometimes the database is pointing at a completely different language. The code asu means one thing to this map (a Bantu language of Tanzania) and another to the databases (a Tupian language of Brazil). Two others collide by name rather than by code: there is a Bulu in Cameroon and a Bulu in Papua New Guinea, and a Mende in Sierra Leone and a Mende in Papua New Guinea. Each pair is unrelated, and each pair nearly ended up sharing a word.
  • The site now has a small tool that settles this in one command: it prints the map's existing words for a language beside the same database's words for the same forty things. If they match, the source is trustworthy. If half of them differ, it is a different language, and no amount of confidence about the single word you wanted will save you.
2026-08-29 — Most of the world has no word for snow, and proving it took the longest 🌨️
  • Snow was the hardest word on the map, and most of the effort went into establishing absences rather than filling cells. Around twenty-four languages have a dictionary that covers ice, hail and frost and simply has no entry for snow. Roughly sixty more can be identified a different way: when a translator needs “white as snow” and the language has no snow, they reach for something else. The substitutions on this map include egret, milk, cotton, kaolin, hailstone and sand.
  • Four reconstructed ancestral languages have no snow either. The oldest recoverable forms of the Afro-Asiatic, Tai, Mayan and Dravidian families each yield a word for hail, hoar-frost, hail again, and dew — and nothing for snow. Proto-Mayan's 1,535-page dictionary contains the word zero times, which is why every Mayan snow word on the map is a later coinage in the highlands: Tojolabal's is literally white land, and K'iche's is built from white plus cold.
  • Where languages do coin one, they mostly describe it. Huasteca Nahuatl says star-ice. Hausa says chaff of ice. Malagasy says rain of hoar-frost. Khanty, Mansi and Nenets have no simple noun for “bird” either and say winged animal, built on the same stem in all three.
  • Across the Pacific the borrowed word tracks the missionary society, not the language family. Cook Islands Māori, Tuvaluan, Tokelauan and Pukapukan all say kiona, from Greek — the London Missionary Society's sphere. Wallisian and East Futunan say nive, from French, in the Marist sphere. Micronesia took the English word and Guam took the Spanish one. Of 64,413 recorded Polynesian words, exactly two mean snow.
  • The one genuinely indigenous African snow word found anywhere: Samburu ɛ-ŋɨ́náɨ́, glossed “obsidian; snow, known only on the peak of Mount Kenya” — and the mountain's own Maa name means the one of the snow. The languages around Kilimanjaro have nothing comparable.
2026-08-29 — The Hittite word for fish turns out to have been its word for bird 𒋗𒉿𒅖
  • The map had 𒋗𒉿𒅖 šuwaiš in Hittite's “fish” cell. It is the word for bird, and Hittite's word for fish is not known at all. Hittite scribes only ever wrote fish with a borrowed Sumerian sign, so no Hittite pronunciation of it survives. The standard hundred-word list for Old Hittite says exactly that under FISH — “unknown” — and gives suwai- under BIRD. The cell is now empty, which is the honest answer, with the reasoning left in place so nobody puts it back.
  • It was found by noticing that a database's Hittite “bird” was letter-for-letter what the map already had under “fish”. That is the same test that caught Mlabri's mother-for-rain and Kuvi's fish-for-bird, and it now runs automatically over the whole map after every change.
  • The uncomfortable part: the wrong word had been introduced by a consistency review. It arrived in a pass whose entire purpose was cross-checking words against each other. A check that only looks at internal agreement can make a row perfectly self-consistent and perfectly wrong.
  • Four more old errors fixed the same day, all of the same kind — a word carrying the standard-Mandarin pronunciation inside a dialect that does not use it. “Orange” had Beijing's tone in fifteen dialects; “white” had it in nine more; Hokkien's “red” and Nanchang's whole set of one tone class were in the wrong place entirely.
2026-08-29 — Vietnamese gets its old writing back for twelve more words 𡥵𡛔
  • Twelve words now show their chữ Nôm form — the Chinese-derived script Vietnamese used for a thousand years before the Latin alphabet. Four (bốn, trăm, gấu, sữa), five if you count the two-character con gái, plus black, salt, tea, honey, wheel, orange and ninety-nine. Every form was looked up in the Nôm Preservation Foundation's dictionary rather than recalled.

  • One of them never needed a font at all. Vietnamese writes “black” as 顛, an ordinary character every device already has. It had been sitting behind an excuse that did not apply to it.

2026-08-29 — A new check found fifteen oranges wearing Beijing's tone 🍊
  • Every Chinese dialect gives one pitch to each historical tone class. So within one language, every word in the 陽平 class has to carry the same contour — and a row that disagrees with itself has a mistake in it somewhere. Nothing on the site was checking that. Now something is, and the first thing it found was that the word for “orange” carried Beijing's tone in fifteen dialects that do not use it: Chengdu, Wuhan, Jinan, Kunming, Wenzhou and ten more. Each of those rows had four to six other words in the same tone class agreeing with each other, and 橙 alone dissenting — always with the same wrong value, which is what a batch filled without adapting the tone looks like. All fifteen corrected.
  • It also caught one cell in Hokkien. 紅 was written /aŋ˧˥/ while the same row's 魚, 名 and 鹽 all said ˨˦, and both neighbouring Hokkien rows said ˨˦ as well — one cell against five independent agreements. Corrected.
  • Making the check useful meant making it narrow. 2026-08-25 — 99, a number instead of a word 🔢, and the language panel stops downloading the whole atlas
  • The new word is not a word. It is 99. Every other concept here is a thing you can point at; this one is a structure. 99 turns out to be the smallest number that pulls apart four different ways of counting at once, which is why it beats 70, 80 or 17. French quatre-vingt-dix-neuf is 4×20+10+9 while Belgian French nonante-neuf is plain 90+9 — the same language, two systems. Basque laurogeita hemeretzi is 4×20+19 and Breton naontek ha pevar-ugent is 19+4×20, the same arithmetic in the opposite order. Danish nioghalvfems is 9 + “half-fifth score”, 4½×20 — a pattern that only surfaces above 50, so a smaller number would never show it. Latin undecentum and Nepali उनान्सय both mean “one from a hundred”. And Hindi निन्यानवे does not come apart at all: Indo-Aryan memorises all hundred numerals separately.
  • The map is coloured by structure, not by form — eight categories, so you can read the typology without reading a single numeral. It uses the same mechanism the tea and orange maps use for their trade routes, so no new code was needed.
  • Asking what Asia had to add turned up the best find on the map. Khmer for nine is ប្រាំបួន, which is literally ប្រាំ “five” plus បួន “four”. So Khmer 99 is 90 + (5+4): a base-five sub-system living inside a decimal one, which none of the existing categories described. It is not an isolate either — Wolof nine is juróom-ñeent, five-four, the same shape on the other side of the world.

Polish dziewięćdziesiąt, Czech devadesát and Bulgarian деветдесет still show nine times ten; Russian девяносто and Ukrainian дев’яносто do not — the decade has fossilised into a word with no visible nine or ten in it. Japanese 九十九 and Vietnamese chín mươi chín are as transparent as a numeral gets; English ninety, Turkish doksan, Korean 아흔 and Tamil தொண்ணூறு are not.

2026-08-24 — “we”, the map’s first typological word 👥, plus “bone” and a shinjitai that had wandered into the Heian period
  • “we” is the first word here that is about grammar rather than vocabulary. About a third of the world’s languages split the first person plural in two: an inclusive “we” meaning me-and-you, and an exclusive “we” meaning me-and-them-but-not-you. Indonesian kita vs kami, Vietnamese chúng ta vs chúng tôi, Tok Pisin yumi vs mipela, Guaraní ñande vs ore. Say the wrong one and you have either invited someone in or shut them out, with no way to hedge. 222 languages force the choice, 840 use one word, 102 are left uncoloured because the answer could not be established.
  • The rule that shaped the map: it only counts if it is in the free pronouns. Where clusivity lives on the verb instead, the language is shown as having one word — because one word is what a speaker actually says. That is WALS chapter 39A’s own criterion. Grambank’s GB028 cannot settle it: its definition says outright that it is “not restricted to the pronominal system … or through verbal marking”. So all six Iroquoian languages and the Siouan ones are shown as having one word despite Grambank coding them 1 — and their “we” cell is identical to their “I” cell, which is correct rather than a copy-paste error. Eleven more Miyako and Yaeyama cells were corrected from that attestation: “mother” (あんな anna / あっぱ appa), “father” (あっちゃ attʃa), “love” (思い゚ umuɨ / 情き nasaki), “cat” (maju / maja), “one”, Miyako “hello” (んみゃーち is a welcome formula, not an everyday greeting), and a dog’s bark わうわう wauwau added for all three Ryukyuan languages — a form 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 independently confirms for Okinawan. Several of these had been holding Okinawan-shaped values in both southern rows, the same copying pattern behind the earlier errors.
  • A second round from the same reader turned up a clear error in Okinawan “love”: it had been 愛 eː, but 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 lists ?ee as an interjection (“hey”), as “among/of”, and as 藍 — indigo. It is not the word for love. That is now 情き nasaki, the form the reporter uses — the dictionary lists it under 情/なさけ and gives “愛のしるし” as a sense. Miyako and Yaeyama “eat”/“drink” also moved to 食い fai/ɸai and 飲み numi, matching the 食(く)ふ stem their conjugation tables give, and the central vowel ɨ was restored in Yaeyama “fire” (pɨː) and both southern rows’ “heart” (kɨmu).
  • Following that up, Okinawan was re-checked against 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 (Shuri). “Good” was wrong: it had been まし maɕi, but the dictionary lists masi as a noun meaning “better, preferable”, not the adjective. Okinawan for “good” is ゆたしゃん jutaɕaɴ. The verbs were right on the map (食でぃ kadi, 飲でぃ nudi) but recorded under the wrong dictionary forms — they are かぬん kanuN and ぬぬん nunuN, and the dictionary’s own conjugation codes (=maN, =di) confirm the connective.
2026-08-04 — New word: “tea” 🍵 and the cha / te split
  • Added tea across 78 languages — and it tells the most famous story in linguistics. Almost every word for tea traces to one of two routes out of China: the overland Silk Road spread chá (→ chai, чай, çay, شاي, τσάι), while Dutch ships spread the Fujianese tê by sea (→ tea, thé, Tee, teh). A few keep a third, Latin root (Polish herbata). Each label now carries a colour-coded route marker — 🐫 overland · ⛵ by sea · 🌿 Latin — so you can watch the two waves wash across the map, and spot the famous exceptions: Portuguese chá, Armenian թեй, Greek τσάι, Swahili chai.
2026-08-04 — New language: Öömrang (Amrum North Frisian)
  • Added Öömrang, the North Frisian dialect of the island of Amrum off the German North Sea coast — one of only a few hundred remaining speakers. It’s a distinct insular variety, sharply different from mainland North Frisian: “fire” is ial (not iilj), “two” is tau (not twäär), “water” weeder, “father” aatj, “I” ik. Every form was sourced and cross-checked against Wiktionary (Föhr-Amrum), the Öömrang Wikipedia, and dialect grammars. Thanks to @avzaagzonunaada for the suggestion.
