2026-08-26 — 99 goes back three thousand years 𓎎𓐂 𒐕𒌍𒑆
  • 137 languages now, and twelve ways of counting. The second half of the day went backwards in time: eighteen ancient languages, two new categories, and — for the first time on this word — cells that are read straight off a page rather than assembled from parts.
  • Sumerian counts in sixties. 𒐕𒌍𒑆 is ĝeš 60 + ušu 30 + ilimmu 9, and it stacks three systems at once: ten is a subbase, the units are quinary (ilimmu is “five” plus “four”), and two of its decades are subtractive — forty is “less two tens”, fifty is “less ten”. Nothing else on the map counts in sixties.
  • Ancient Egyptian and Coptic sit together, three thousand years apart. 𓎎𓐂 psḏyw psḏ and its descendant ⲡⲥⲧⲁⲓⲟⲩⲯⲓⲥ: the same 90 + 9, with the same decade built off the word for nine. The Coptic one is the best-attested cell on the map — it is read out of the parable of the lost sheep, Matthew 18:12 and Luke 15, where a hundred sheep lose one and “the ninety-nine” are left behind.
  • Old French turns out to be the conservative one. It is nonante et nuef, not quatre-vingt-dix-neuf — so the vigesimal count modern France uses is the innovation, and Belgian and Swiss nonante is the older form kept. And it is attested whole, in verse: Le Couronnement de Louis, line 12, “Quant Deus eslist nonante et nuef reiames” — when God chose ninety-nine kingdoms, he made the best of them into sweet France.
  • Japanese has a word that means ninety-nine and nothing else. Heian つくも, usually derived from 次百 tsugu-momo, “next to a hundred” — it names the number by pointing at the one above it, in a language whose numerals are otherwise strictly additive. 伊勢物語 63 has つくも髪, hair “one year short of a hundred years”. The counting numeral was ここのそあまりここのつ, which is what the Old Japanese row carries.
  • Zulu builds the number nine by subtraction. isishiyagalolunye is “leave one”, isishiyagalombili is “leave two”, and six is isithupha, literally “thumb”. The subtraction is inside the unit rather than applied to the whole numeral, so it gets its own colour — the twelfth.
  • Tibetan tells you which ten you are in before the unit arrives. དགུ་བཅུ་གོ་དགུ is nine-ten plus nine, but the particle joining them differs for every decade: རྩ for 21–29, སོ for 31–39, and so on down to གོ for 91–99. The linker carries the decade.
  • Cornish nownsek ha peswar ugens and Manx kiare feed as nuy-jeig put the Celtic count-by-twenties on both sides of the Irish Sea with Breton, and Haitian Creole carries the French version to the Caribbean.
  • Ancient languages also deepened the categories that had been thinnest. Old English nigon and hundnigontig and Sanskrit navanavati both put the unit first, which makes the German and Dutch habit look old rather than quirky; Old Norse and Old Church Slavonic say “nine tens” outright; Biblical Hebrew and Syriac pluralise the nine exactly as Hebrew, Arabic and Maltese still do.
  • A full review of the word found one real error. Swedish had been filed as having a fossilised decade, on the strength of nittio read alone. The series gives it away — femtio is fem + tio, åttio is åtta + tio — so tio “ten” is visible in every Swedish decade and only the nine is reduced. Filed with the transparent group now. Latin was also wrong and is fixed to ūndēcentum; that row writes macrons throughout and the form had been taken from a page title, which strips them.
  • The colour key now hides categories the current era has none of — Classical Nahuatl is the only base-twenty-on-fifteen language and is historical-only, so on the modern map that row had a swatch and no dots to point at.
2026-08-26 — 99 grows to a hundred languages and ten ways of counting 🔢
  • The 99 map went from 51 languages to 100, and from six categories to ten. It is coloured by the structure of the numeral rather than its sound, so what you are looking at is a map of how people build a number, not of what it sounds like.
  • The biggest change is a split inside “decimal”. Putting Japanese 九十九 next to Russian девяносто девять in one colour was hiding the difference: 九十九 is nine-ten-nine, every part visible, while девяносто is a word with no nine and no ten in it at all. The test is whether the language’s ordinary word for ten is still there in the decade. It divides Slavic down the middle — Polish dziewięćdziesiąt, Czech devadesát and Bulgarian деветдесет transparent, Russian and Belarusian not — and it puts English ninety, Turkish doksan, Korean 아흔 and Tamil தொண்ணூறு on the fossilised side.
  • Turkic divides the same way, one against six. Tatar туксан, Bashkir туҡһан, Kyrgyz токсон, Turkmen togsan, Azerbaijani doxsan, Uyghur توقسان and Turkish doksan all come from *tokuz + on, nine and ten, fused past recognition. Chuvash alone kept them apart: тӑхӑрвуннӑ, тӑхӑр “nine” plus вуннӑ “ten”.
  • Three languages make their ninety by pluralising nine. Hebrew תשעים, Arabic تسعون and Maltese disgħin use the ordinary Semitic plural ending — a decade that is literally “nines”. Nothing else on the map does it, so it has its own colour.
  • Classical Nahuatl counts on fifteen. 99 is nāppōhualli oncaxtōlli onnāhui: 4×20 + 15 + 4. Vigesimal like Basque and Breton, but where those finish with nineteen, Nahuatl finishes on caxtōlli, and its nine is chiucnāhui, five-and-four. Three bases stacked in one numeral.
  • Odia shows subtraction better than any other language here. 98 is ଅଠାନବେ, built like every other number in the nineties — and then 99 switches to ଅନେଶୋତ, “one less than a hundred”. The subtraction is not a system running through the language; it is a single hole immediately under the round number. Which is the whole reason for choosing 99 rather than 80 or 70.
  • Twenty-two Chinese varieties arrived at once, and the tones were derived rather than looked up. The sounds come from per-county rhyme tables, but the tones come from each language’s own row: 九 and 水 belong to the same Middle Chinese tone class, and so do 十 and 白. Two characters in one class carry the same modern tone in any dialect — Mandarin included, where the old entering tone is gone but 十 and 白 went the same way together. So every tone on those 22 rows is anchored to a cell that was already there, and none had to be guessed. Five varieties were dropped rather than forced, where that anchor did not hold.
  • Also in: Burmese, Javanese, Sundanese and Minangkabau; Maltese, Afrikaans and Esperanto — which is naŭdek naŭ, nine-ten-nine, exactly what a language built to be regular looks like beside its neighbours; and Slovene, Slovak, Croatian, Serbian, Bosnian, Macedonian, Latvian, Catalan, Galician and Occitan.
  • Two cells say “inferred” on the map rather than pretending. Yoruba and Nahuatl are composed from a documented rule and documented parts, because no source spells the whole numeral out. The map marks them, rather than this note doing it.
  • The colour key now sits above the credit line instead of behind it, and clicking a row in it blinks every language in that category twice — with ten colours, hunting for one of them across a world map had stopped being reasonable.
  • A sweep comparing each new cell’s phonetic symbols against every other cell in the same language caught fifteen transcription slips — Vietnamese writing one vowel two ways, Polish a bare e in a row that has none, Nanning Cantonese given a sound it does not have. All corrected to what the row already used.
2026-08-25 — 99, a number instead of a word 🔢, and the language panel stops downloading the whole atlas
  • The new word is not a word. It is 99. Every other concept here is a thing you can point at; this one is a structure. 99 turns out to be the smallest number that pulls apart four different ways of counting at once, which is why it beats 70, 80 or 17. French quatre-vingt-dix-neuf is 4×20+10+9 while Belgian French nonante-neuf is plain 90+9 — the same language, two systems. Basque laurogeita hemeretzi is 4×20+19 and Breton naontek ha pevar-ugent is 19+4×20, the same arithmetic in the opposite order. Danish nioghalvfems is 9 + “half-fifth score”, 4½×20 — a pattern that only surfaces above 50, so a smaller number would never show it. Latin undecentum and Nepali उनान्सय both mean “one from a hundred”. And Hindi निन्यानवे does not come apart at all: Indo-Aryan memorises all hundred numerals separately.
  • The map is coloured by structure, not by form — eight categories, so you can read the typology without reading a single numeral. It uses the same mechanism the tea and orange maps use for their trade routes, so no new code was needed.
  • Asking what Asia had to add turned up the best find on the map. Khmer for nine is ប្រាំបួន, which is literally ប្រាំ “five” plus បួន “four”. So Khmer 99 is 90 + (5+4): a base-five sub-system living inside a decimal one, which none of the existing categories described. It is not an isolate either — Wolof nine is juróom-ñeent, five-four, the same shape on the other side of the world.
  • A split inside Slavic that a single “decimal” colour had been hiding. Polish dziewięćdziesiąt, Czech devadesát and Bulgarian деветдесет still show nine times ten; Russian девяносто and Ukrainian дев’яносто do not — the decade has fossilised into a word with no visible nine or ten in it. Japanese 九十九 and Vietnamese chín mươi chín are as transparent as a numeral gets; English ninety, Turkish doksan, Korean 아흔 and Tamil தொண்ணூறு are not.
  • 51 languages so far, and it will stay a partial map on purpose. “five” reaches 857 rows out of 1,164 and that is the ceiling — a great many languages have no native numeral this high and borrow above five or ten. The blank areas are the finding. Two cells are marked as inferred rather than attested, and say so on the map: Yoruba, whose numerals subtract throughout — even 90 is 20×5−10 — and Wolof.
  • Opening a language used to download the entire word corpus. The word files are organised by concept — one file holds “water” for all 1,164 languages — which is exactly the shape the map wants and exactly the wrong shape for the language panel, which needs one language’s whole row. So the first click on any language pulled all 55 files, 695 KB, to display 55 cells. That is now about 1 KB. Same for the compare panel.
  • Snow reached 705 languages, and a question that had been open for weeks got an answer from the map itself. Should a borrowed word count? It already did — Hindi बर्फ़, Somali baraf, Swahili theluji, Indonesian salju, Tagalog niyebe are all loans and all the word their speakers actually use. So the rule is the mainstream everyday word, whatever its origin.
  • Mayan languages will not be getting a snow word. Kaufman and Justeson’s 1,535-page comparative dictionary reconstructs none at any level, and its weather section runs rain → sky → thunder → hail → wind. Every “snow” in a Mayan dictionary is a frost or hail word pressed into service — which is why Tzotzil taiv, glossed “nieve” by its own dictionary, is left out: it is the Proto-Mayan word for frost.
  • New snow entries include Tshangla phom, Korana abas, Menominee kōn, Wichita hiraːʔa, Rukai pasaabu, Fur umé and Eastern Huasteca Nahuatl sepayauitl. Two of those corrected a source rather than confirming one: the widely-copied Wichita form was this noun with its ending cut off, and a published Fur word list had snow glossed from the neighbouring dictionary entry — a salt that forms on the ground.
  • Three Goryeo Korean cells were reading a Chinese character as if it were Korean. The 1103 Jilin leishi writes 水曰沒 — “water is called mwol” — but it also has entries where the gloss just repeats the headword, and those record no Korean word at all. 赤曰赤 for “red” is one, in a colour list where the compiler did write down real Korean for white and yellow. Those cells are now blank, which is the honest answer.
  • Smaller repairs: the Miyako and Yaeyama words for “red” and “good” had stray katakana in otherwise-hiragana forms; the pronunciation column in the language panel no longer wraps everything in slashes, which were wrong on any row the map itself labels as a romanisation; and a new checker catches tone written as digits in an IPA field, which nothing had been looking for.
2026-08-24 — “we”, the map’s first typological word 👥, plus “bone” and a shinjitai that had wandered into the Heian period
  • “we” is the first word here that is about grammar rather than vocabulary. About a third of the world’s languages split the first person plural in two: an inclusive “we” meaning me-and-you, and an exclusive “we” meaning me-and-them-but-not-you. Indonesian kita vs kami, Vietnamese chúng ta vs chúng tôi, Tok Pisin yumi vs mipela, Guaraní ñande vs ore. Say the wrong one and you have either invited someone in or shut them out, with no way to hedge. 222 languages force the choice, 840 use one word, 102 are left uncoloured because the answer could not be established.
  • The rule that shaped the map: it only counts if it is in the free pronouns. Where clusivity lives on the verb instead, the language is shown as having one word — because one word is what a speaker actually says. That is WALS chapter 39A’s own criterion. Grambank’s GB028 cannot settle it: its definition says outright that it is “not restricted to the pronominal system … or through verbal marking”. So all six Iroquoian languages and the Siouan ones are shown as having one word despite Grambank coding them 1 — and their “we” cell is identical to their “I” cell, which is correct rather than a copy-paste error. The same is true of 我 in Old, Middle and Classical Chinese, where it is simply number-neutral.
  • Non-obvious splits, each sourced: Southern Min 咱 lán vs 阮 goán; northern Mandarin 咱们 vs 我们, while Taiwan carries the marker for “no distinction”; Tungusic throughout — Evenki мит/бу, Manchu ᠮᡠᠰᡝ/ᠪᡝ; Gujarati આપણે/અમે and Marathi आपण/आम्ही as the Indo-European exceptions; Classical Mongolian bida/ba even though modern Mongolian has one word.
  • Four rows were left blank on purpose. Aghem and Meta’ need Hyman (1979) and an unpublished set of notes; Koyukon needs a print dictionary; Azhe has no entry in any comparative database at all. Every SIL-Cameroon PDF for the first two was read through the Wayback Machine before giving up. A blank is the honest answer there.
  • “bone” — the hardest word in the body to borrow. Almost every row is inherited, and Germanic and Slavic each threw the inherited word away independently: Germanic for *bainą, literally “the straight thing” and the same word as German Bein “leg”; Slavic for *kostь. Korean dialect “bone” is a different word rather than a sound change — Gyeongsang 삐, Jeju 꽝 — and Middle Korean is ᄲᅧ, whose initial letter is the sp- cluster that modern ㅃ came from.
  • A reader-reported error, and the class behind it. 猫 is a 20th-century simplified form: it cannot appear in Heian, medieval, Edo or kanbun Japanese, and certainly not in chữ Nôm, which predates it by centuries. Five cells now read 貓. Checking for the same mistake elsewhere found 歯 in four historical Japanese rows, now 齒.
  • Fonts gained three characters so they stop rendering as blank boxes on phones: 𩩫 (Nôm xương), 𫣆 (the Taiwanese Hakka inclusive “we”), and the Old Hangul letters ᄲ and ᅧ for Middle Korean.
2026-08-23 — The map shows up fast now, even on slow Wi-Fi ⚡
  • On a slow connection the words used to appear, then vanish for minutes before coming back. The first tap on a language was quietly pulling down an enormous amount: a 19 MB file holding every language’s full description, 5 MB of interface translations for all 22 UI languages at once, and the country borders streamed from an outside server at full resolution — tens of megabytes, one file at a time.
  • Now the map loads what you actually need, when you need it. Opening a language pulls a ~1 MB summary of every language plus the ~10 KB description of just the one you opened. Interface text loads only your language — about 0.4 MB for Japanese, and nothing at all for English, since English is the original. The country outlines are now self-hosted and simplified, 14.6 MB → 1.9 MB, so they no longer freeze the map while they draw.
  • Nothing was thrown away. Every language still carries its complete description and sources, and every interface language its full set of translations — the exact same text as before, verified character-for-character. It is only split up so a phone fetches the slice in front of you instead of the entire library on the first tap.
  • The family-tree page and the Han-character map got the same treatment — the tree was loading that 19 MB file plus all 22 languages’ translations just to draw the branches, and both pages were pulling every UI language’s translations at once; now each loads only the small summary and your own interface language.
2026-08-22 — Eight new words at once, and a way to add them without inventing anything 🐦
  • Eight concepts in one go, taking the Word Map to 53 words: snow ❄️, stone 🪨, nose 👃, bird 🐦, egg 🥚, sleep 😴, white ⚪ and five 5️⃣. Each carries a full set of interface labels and a sense-disambiguated definition, and each already spans several hundred languages (from ~440 to ~740).
  • How they were built. Instead of scraping a dictionary site language by language — which stalls the moment a minority language has no IPA online — each word was generated from the model’s own knowledge in two or more independent passes, and a cell was kept only where the passes agreed on the same form. Disagreement is exactly the signature of a guessed word, so the cross-check throws those out. Nothing was filled that could not be corroborated twice.
  • Indo-European never settled on a word for “bird”. English bird is from Old English bridd (originally a nestling), German took Vogel, Latin avis went to Romance, Slavic says ptica — four unrelated roots for one everyday animal, against Sino-Tibetan 鳥 and Austronesian *manuk.
  • Five is a hand. Austronesian *lima means both, and family after family builds “five” out of the word for the hand — a fossil of counting on fingers. White and black, meanwhile, are the first two colour terms any language lexicalises (Berlin & Kay), which is why white travels so far so cleanly. And egg is PIE *h₂ōwyóm — English egg is actually a Norse import that displaced the native ey.
2026-08-21 — The historic scripts stop being tofu on phones 西夏 突厥
  • The map named the right fonts for its oldest scripts — “Noto Serif Tangut”, “Noto Sans Old Turkic”, and two dozen others — but those are desktop system fonts. iPhones, most Androids and Windows ship almost none of them, so Tangut 西夏, Old Turkic 突厥, Egyptian, Cuneiform, Yi, Vai, Mongolian, Coptic, Phoenician, Ugaritic, Linear B and a dozen more rendered as rows of □□□ on a phone. A coverage checker only proved the name was in the stylesheet, never that the glyph would actually appear, so it stayed green while the phone showed boxes.
  • Now 21 historic scripts are self-hosted — each cut down to exactly the characters the data uses (about 120 KB in total, loaded only when its script is on screen), so they render the same on a phone as on a desktop.
  • The same sweep fixed the Chữ Nôm forms of the new words — 𦖻 (ear), 𩄎 (rain), 𩙋 (wind), 𡐙 (earth) — and the Old Hangul jamo of their Middle-Korean readings, which had been tofu on iOS.
  • Smaller thing, same cause: the tea route legend no longer has its bottom row clipped under the iPhone home-indicator bar.
  • In the background the four newest core words kept filling out — wind alone went from 423 to 743 languages — by the same generate-and-cross-check method.
2026-08-20 — Five more words — ear, rain, wind, earth — and one that isn’t polite 🧪
  • Four core words — ear 👂, rain 🌧️, wind 🌬️, earth 🟫 — plus one experimental one kept off to the side and marked 🧪: poop. Earth here is the soil underfoot, the substance you dig, not the planet and not a country — the split that trips up half the world’s dictionaries.
  • The Indo-European anchors are old and stable: ear is PIE *h₂ṓws (Latin auris, Greek οὖς), wind is *h₂weh₁-nt-, literally “blowing”. Elsewhere the roots go their own way — Semitic rīḥ is both “wind” and “spirit”, Bantu *-pepo gives Swahili upepo, and “earth = soil” runs on entirely separate words from “earth = world”.
  • Each of the five then went through review rallies — vocabulary, ancient languages, a delicate Japanese/Korean dialect pass, and an IPA-notation sweep — that corrected well over a hundred cells across the batch.
  • One of those checks paid off later: the experimental poop word had been given a Proto-Koreanic reconstruction, *sotwonko, that no source supports and that doesn’t fit its own attested descendant (Middle Korean ᄯᅩᇰ). With nothing to cite, the cell was removed rather than kept — the Proto-Japonic *kuso beside it is real and stayed.
2026-08-19 — A new core word: “tooth”, a noun that is really a verb
  • The 40th concept, across all 1,151 languages: tooth, one tooth, the ordinary kind.
  • PIE *h₃dónts is the present participle of *h₁ed- “to eat”. The tooth is literally “the one that eats” — so this row and the map’s “eat” row are the same word twice over: Latin dēns, Greek ὀδούς, Sanskrit दन्तः, Lithuanian dantis, Old Irish dét, Gothic 𐍄𐌿𐌽𐌸𐌿𐍃, English tooth, German Zahn.
  • Elsewhere the map cuts differently. Semitic keeps *šinn- from Akkadian 𒅆𒅔𒉡 to Arabic سن and Geʽez ስን; Bantu *-jìnò gives izinyo, iino, leino, dinu; Turkic *dīĺ runs diş / тіс / тиис from the Aegean to the Lena; Austronesian *nipen reaches Taiwan (nipun) and the Philippines (ngipin) while Malay took gigi instead.
  • The Sinosphere cuts the mouth in two: 牙 is the back tooth and the tusk, 齒 the front tooth. Every Chinese variety on the map says 牙 — but Japanese says 歯, Korean 이, Vietnamese răng, and none of the three borrowed the Chinese word.
  • Old Japanese gets its p- back: 歯 pa, not ha — and Miyako and Yaeyama still say ぱー, the sound the mainland lost. Goryeo Korean has a second Jilin leishi entry, 齒曰你, so “tooth” is 你 ni where “tongue” was 蝎.
  • Sourced against ASJP’s 40-item core, where tooth is the sixth best-attested concept on earth (10,715 languages) — better covered than “fish” or “tree”, both of which the map already carries. The Nôm font subset gained 𪘵.
2026-08-19 — Five review rallies over the four new words, and a Goryeo tongue with an -l on it
  • Blood, tongue, night and orange went through five rallies. Each asked a different question, and the two that mattered most were the ones aimed at the places that get read hardest: Japanese/Korean dialects and ancient languages.
  • Rally 1 — Japonic and Koreanic. The best find is in Goryeo Korean: the Jilin leishi (1103) records 舌曰蝎, and the reading is 혈 — with an -l that Middle Korean had already lost by the 15th century. Goryeo “tongue” is no longer blank, and Proto-Koreanic was corrected from *hyə to *hyəl on the strength of it. Miyako and Yaeyama “tongue” were rewritten to the spellings those rows already use for the apical vowel (すぃた), and Old Japanese “night” went from yoru to yo, the actual free form of the period.
  • The Korean dialect rows say 쎄 for tongue in Jeolla and Gyeongsang, not 혀 — the ㅎ→ㅅ change that also gives 형→성 and 힘→심, and that survives in the phrase 쎄빠지게. Jeju says 세. Hamgyong, in the north-east, keeps 혀.
  • Rally 2 — ancient languages. Every cuneiform, hieroglyphic and Avestan spelling was checked sign by sign. Two real errors: Hittite “blood” was spelled with 𒌍, which is the sign for thirty, and Pyu “night” had been filled from a Taman wordlist — a different language entirely. Proto-Afro-Asiatic “night” was withdrawn as well: *layl- reaches Proto-Semitic and no further.
  • Rally 3 — a deterministic checker, not a judgement call. Comparing each new cell against the writing system its own row already uses found 21 cells in the wrong script — Yaghnobi and Luri written in Cyrillic and Arabic where the row is Latin, Aleut and Yupik in Latin where the row is Cyrillic, Western Cham in Latin where the row is Akhar Jawi. One of them, Talysh “blood”, turned out to be a fused-script word: an Arabic خ followed by Cyrillic ун.
  • Rally 4 — the fabrication test. 393 groups of languages sharing an identical form were examined for the signature that caught the Igbo-word-in-five-Edoid-languages error in July. Nothing new: every group turned out to be shared script (血, ཁྲག), a real dialect continuum (Manding su), or a genuinely identical inheritance (Polynesian pō, Philippine dila). One cell was replaced anyway — Edo “tongue” had been projected from its Edoid neighbours, and a real word list gives arranmwẹn.
  • Rally 5 — the orange map. Two policy problems: Classical Latin and Old Irish had been given words for a fruit that had not reached them yet (both now blank, while Classical Persian gained نارنج — that route starts there), and 105 language varieties were missing because a partial word does not inherit from parent to variant. Orange is now 375 cells.
2026-08-19 — A new word: “orange”, the fruit that arrived twice and got four names 🍊
  • A new partial word (🧪), in 272 languages, and only the second map with a route marker — the colouring the “tea” map uses for the cha/te split. Orange needs four colours instead of two.
  • The fruit reached Europe twice. The bitter orange came overland with Persian and Arab trade in the Middle Ages, carrying Sanskrit नारङ्ग with it — نارنج → Spanish naranja, Italian arancia, French and English orange, Polish pomarańcza, Hungarian narancs, Armenian նարինջ. Two hundred years later the sweet orange arrived by sea on Portuguese ships, and half the eastern Mediterranean simply named it after the traders: Greek πορτοκάλι, Turkish portakal, Arabic برتقال, Amharic ብርቱካን, Georgian ფორთოხალი, Persian پرتقال.
  • Northern Europe took a third answer from Dutch merchants, who called it the “apple from China”: sinaasappel, Low German Apelsien, Russian апельсин, Finnish appelsiini, Icelandic appelsína. The same idea reached Morocco independently through French la Chine — Moroccan Arabic لتشين, Algerian تشينة.

Konso has no voiced stops and no ejectives — it has the rare implosive series ɓ ɗ ʄ ʛ instead, including a uvular implosive almost no other language has. Yet the pronunciation column was full of /b/, /d/, /ɡ/, /dʒ/ and /kʼ/. Six cells could be flagged as impossible without knowing a word of either language. Konso now reads tika (house), kuta (dog), apitta (fire), ʛoyra (tree), aayyaa (mother), sataʔta (heart) — each cited to a page of the grammar.
  • Comanche had two cells holding the same word: “good” and “moon” were both puha, which actually means “medicine, spiritual power”. Fixed to tsaatʉ and mʉa. Worse, “hello” was haʊ — the English-language “how” stereotype of Plains Indian speech, not a Comanche word at all. The real greeting is marʉawe, and “thank you” (ʉra), which had been blank, is now filled. Also corrected: dog (sarii), house (kahni), tree (huupi), mother (pia), father (ahpʉ).
  • Where no source could be found, the cell now says so rather than guessing. Comanche “heart” is blank; “cat” and “love” are kept but flagged as unverified; Konso “moon” uses the word for “month” and is marked as an inference, because the grammar has no entry for “moon” anywhere in its 900 pages.
  • Hopi “tree” was this row’s own word for “one” with the glottal stop dropped (suukya’ → suukya). Now hotski.
2026-08-06 — Five languages were on the map twice
  • Somali, Iñupiaq, Buryat, Onondaga and Khmu each had two rows at the same coordinates, so one pin was drawn directly on top of the other and the duplicate was invisible. Consolidated: 1,138 → 1,135 languages. No word was lost — every cell the removed row filled, the surviving row already filled.
  • Two of them were a different problem from a plain duplicate. The Onondaga row used the code onn, which in ISO 639-3 is Onobasulu, a language of Papua New Guinea; and the Khmu row used xkk, which is Kachok, a Bahnaric language of Cambodia. Both are now on their correct codes (ono and kjg) and keep their translated names in all 19 interface languages.
  • “Cree (Plains)” is renamed simply Cree. It is the macrolanguage, and Plains Cree is a separate row hanging off it — the old name made the parent look like a rival for the child’s slot.
2026-08-06 — “Official in” now says what is actually true
  • For 124 languages the “Official in” field held nothing but a country name. For Japanese or Thai that is correct. For Berom, Tiv and Efik it said “Nigeria”, which reads as though they are official languages of Nigeria — they are not; English is. Same for Konso and Gedeo (“Ethiopia”), and for Northern Qiang, Azhe, Hani, Xibe and Jiamao (“China”).

Where a country grants a real but partial status, the field now says which: Mexico’s sixteen indigenous rows cite the 2003 law that makes them national languages with the same validity as Spanish in their own territory; Guatemala’s five cite the Ley de Idiomas Nacionales; Colombia and Venezuela cite the constitutional articles making indigenous languages official within their own territories. Chipewyan keeps “Canada” — Dëne Sųłiné really is one of the 11 official languages of the Northwest Territories. Nheengatu is co-official in one Amazonian municipality and now says so.
  • Australia’s four languages now state the fact that surprises people: Australia has no official language at federal level. Swiss German no longer claims to be official in Switzerland (the official written language is Standard German). And Ancient Egyptian no longer says “Official in: Ancient Egypt”.

2026-08-06 — Han Map: 口 was “mouth”, 立 was “stand”
  • Five Han Map cells held a different character’s reading. Dungan — the Sinitic language of Central Asia written in Cyrillic — gave 口 as зуйъ, which is 嘴 “mouth”, and 立 as җань, which is 站 “to stand”: in both cases the everyday word for the concept instead of the character’s own reading. The Cyrillic for 行 was 走’s, copied one row over.
  • Pu-Xian Min 三 was wrong three times at once. 三 is a 心母 character, which in Pu-Xian gives the language’s celebrated /ɬ/ — the same initial its own 四, 山, 心 and 西 already had; the nasal coda was missing; and the tone was the wrong one. Meixian Hakka 坐 was on the 上聲 tone, missing the classic Hakka shift of 全濁上 to 陰平 that the other four Hakka varieties all show.
  • Middle Korean 食 used ᅀ, the letter Dongguk Jeongun reserves for the 日母 initial — while 十, the same kind of syllable, was already correct with ㅆ. And the Korean native-reading row for 北 showed 북, which is the Sino-Korean reading, next to its own native gloss 된 (as in 된바람, the north wind).
2026-08-04 — Miyako and Yaeyama “eat”/“drink” corrected (reader feedback)
  • Thanks to @Soda_Limer on X, who pointed out that the Japonic varieties stay clearly distinguishable in their plain dictionary form. Following that up turned out to expose a real error: Yaeyama had been classified as Northern Ryukyuan and given Okinawan’s forms verbatim, which is why Okinawan and Yaeyama were showing identical cells. Yaeyama is in fact Southern Ryukyuan, most closely related to Miyako. Both southern rows are now on sourced forms: Miyako “eat” is foː (食ー) and Yaeyama “eat” is ɸoːŋ (食ーん) — f/h-initial reflexes of 食(く)ふ, quite unlike Okinawan’s 噛む-based word. Miyako “drink” is back to numu. Yaeyama “drink” is still an Okinawan copy and is now flagged as provisional until a dictionary source can be checked.
  • The connective-form rule for the mainland dialects is unchanged. It earns its keep on “drink”, where the plain form is 飲む in all seven mainland varieties — no contrast at all — while the connective splits them into 飲んで and Kyushu’s 飲うで.
  • “Good” is now sourced for all three Ryukyuan languages through 琉球大学’s 大琉球語辞典, which cross-searches seven Ryukyuan dictionaries at once. All three had been showing まし maɕi — a noun meaning “preferable”, not the adjective. They are now Okinawan ゆたさん jutasaɴ, Miyako ぞーはン dzoːhaɴ (伊良部方言辞典) and Yaeyama カイシャーン kaiʃaːɴ (石垣方言辞典).
  • A third round, this time with the reader identifying themselves as a speaker of these languages rather than as someone quoting a dictionary. Eleven more Miyako and Yaeyama cells were corrected from that attestation: “mother” (あんな anna / あっぱ appa), “father” (あっちゃ attʃa), “love” (思い゚ umuɨ / 情き nasaki), “cat” (maju / maja), “one”, Miyako “hello” (んみゃーち is a welcome formula, not an everyday greeting), and a dog’s bark わうわう wauwau added for all three Ryukyuan languages — a form 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 independently confirms for Okinawan. Several of these had been holding Okinawan-shaped values in both southern rows, the same copying pattern behind the earlier errors.
  • A second round from the same reader turned up a clear error in Okinawan “love”: it had been 愛 eː, but 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 lists ?ee as an interjection (“hey”), as “among/of”, and as 藍 — indigo. It is not the word for love. That is now 情き nasaki, the form the reporter uses — the dictionary lists it under 情/なさけ and gives “愛のしるし” as a sense. Miyako and Yaeyama “eat”/“drink” also moved to 食い fai/ɸai and 飲み numi, matching the 食(く)ふ stem their conjugation tables give, and the central vowel ɨ was restored in Yaeyama “fire” (pɨː) and both southern rows’ “heart” (kɨmu).

Okinawan for “good” is ゆたしゃん jutaɕaɴ. The verbs were right on the map (食でぃ kadi, 飲でぃ nudi) but recorded under the wrong dictionary forms — they are かぬん kanuN and ぬぬん nunuN, and the dictionary’s own conjugation codes (=maN, =di) confirm the connective.
2026-08-04 — New word: “tea” 🍵 and the cha / te split
  • Added tea across 78 languages — and it tells the most famous story in linguistics. Almost every word for tea traces to one of two routes out of China: the overland Silk Road spread chá (→ chai, чай, çay, شاي, τσάι), while Dutch ships spread the Fujianese tê by sea (→ tea, thé, Tee, teh). A few keep a third, Latin root (Polish herbata). Each label now carries a colour-coded route marker — 🐫 overland · ⛵ by sea · 🌿 Latin — so you can watch the two waves wash across the map, and spot the famous exceptions: Portuguese chá, Armenian թեй, Greek τσάι, Swahili chai.
