2026-11-04 — Hokkien towns
  • Word Map: eight words in the Xiamen, Zhangzhou, Quanzhou and Teochew rows, each the town's word from Wiktionary's dialect tables with Wiktionary's own pronunciation for that town: the dragonfly 田嬰 (chhân-ni in Xiamen, chhân-eⁿ in Zhangzhou, chhân-iⁿ in Quanzhou), the Milky Way 河溪 and the little finger 尾指仔. Teochew gets the Milky Way 天河, the "heavenly river", and the shooting star 流星 in the same way.
2026-11-03 — Khmer goosebumps, Hokkien 99
  • Word Map: two more words in Southeast Asian rows, each from the same word list as the rest of its row: the Bulang bear khih, which matches Wa krih, and the Idu Mishmi word for person.
  • Word Map: North Korean gets dragonfly 잠자리, hiccup 딸꾹질, thumb 엄지손가락, rainbow 무지개 and thunder 우뢰, the North's standard spelling of 우레, respelled after a folk etymology "rain-thunder".
  • Word Map: 47 words in Chinese dialects, each the word Wiktionary's dialect tables give for that town, read from the same MCPDict survey table the row already follows. The dragonfly is the "pond tail" 塘尾 in Zhongshan, the "yellow tail" 黄尾 in Taishan and the "river spider" 河蟢蟢 in Xinzhou; the bat in Wuyuan is 老鼠蒲翼, a "mouse butterfly"; and a shooting star in Lüliang is 走星, the "running star", and its bat 夜壁虎, the "night gecko". In Hengyang the little finger is 满手脑, the "youngest" finger. Grandmothers follow the dialect tables for both sides: Jinhua's father's mother is 妈妈 and her mother's mother 外婆, while Nanning, like Zhongshan, says 阿婆 for both.
  • Word Map: the Osaka, Kyoto, Hakata and Hiroshima rows, and the Jeolla and Busan Korean rows, now show the national word where every region says the same thing — country names, whale, octopus, shooting star, rainbow, Milky Way, thumb and little finger, bat, pineapple, tomato, sunflower and turkey — so these cities no longer drop off the map for those words. Words with known regional forms, like thunder or hiccup, are left for a source.
  • Word Map: a reader, Zheng Jing-Yan, confirmed that Hokkien says káu-tsa̍p-káu for 99 in speech, as the map has it, and kiú-si̍p-kiú only as a literary reading; the Taiwanese note now says so.
  • Word Map: a Khmer reader pointed out that ព្រឺ, "to get goosebumps", usually comes with the part that bristles: ព្រឺរោម (body hair), ព្រឺស្បែក (skin), ព្រឺក្បាល (head) or ព្រឺសក់ (head hair). The Khmer note now lists them.
2026-11-03 — Old languages: Spain, Frisia, Armenia and the Book of Rites
  • Word Map: 131 more words in historical languages. The Old Spanish bat is murciego, the "blind mouse", attested about 1250; Old Frisian thunder is thuner, and Old Spanish and Old Frisian, like Latin, have only the general word for "brother". Classical Armenian thunder is որոտ and its whale կէտ, borrowed from Greek κῆτος. Middle Irish Monday is lúan, the Moon. The Greeks are यवनाः Yavanas in Sanskrit, from Old Persian Yauna, "Ionian". The jellyfish is the "sea lung" in both Latin, pulmō marīnus, and Greek, πλεύμων, and the Middle Chinese hiccup is 噦, which the Book of Rites forbids in front of one's parents. The Nahuatl little finger is mahpilxōcoyōtl, the "youngest child" finger, and the Syriac one ܚܨܪܐ comes from a root "to narrow". Gothic's only word for grandmother, 𐌰𐍅𐍉, is used once, for Timothy's grandmother Lois. Ottoman Turkish has آغا ağa for an older brother, and the Old Turkic inscriptions call China 𐱃𐰉𐰍𐰲 Tabgač, after the Tuoba of Northern Wei. Syriac adds China ܨܝܢ and Coptic India ϩⲉⲛⲧⲟⲩ. The Aztec Milky Way is citlālīcue, "her skirt of stars", which Molina's 1555 dictionary glosses as the Camino de Santiago; the Syriac shooting star ܢܝܙܟܐ is also a "spear"; and sugar is śakkār in Tocharian B but gula, from Sanskrit guḍa, in Old Javanese. In Ugarit thunder is 𐎅𐎄 Haddu, the storm god himself; the Aramaic and Hebrew whale is the תנין, the sea monster that the King James Bible calls the "great whales" of Genesis 1:21; Classical Persian has the bat شب‌پره, the "night-wing", and the Pali name for India is जम्बुदीप, the "rose-apple continent". The Old Irish butterfly etelachán is the "little flying creature", the Old East Slavic little finger мѣзиньць was the "youngest son", Classical Armenian Monday երկշաբաթի is the "second of the week", and Old Frisian morgen means both morning and tomorrow. From Gerhard Köbler's dictionaries: the Old Saxon bat fletharmūs, the "flutter-mouse", found once in an 11th-century glossary; the Old Frisian rainbow reinboga; and the Old Frisian little finger slūtere, the "locker" that closes the fist. The Old and Middle High German little finger is ōrfingar, ōrvinger, the "ear finger", like Latin auriculāris. Sanskrit's little finger कनिष्ठिका is "the smallest" and its thunder स्तनयित्नु, personified in the plural as the children of Lightning, goes back to the Rigveda (Monier-Williams); the Pali bat वग्गुलि is the "flutterer", and Pali जेट्ठ, the first-born, is the elder brother (Pali Text Society dictionary). From the Comprehensive Aramaic Lexicon: the Syriac thumb ܟܪܬܐ and hiccup ܦܘܩܬܐ, and the Aramaic thumb ܐܠܝܘܢܐ of Targum Onkelos, little finger ܚܨܪܐ, silk ܫܐܪܝܐ and Monday ܬܪܝܢ ܒܫܒܐ, "two in the week". Ancient Greek's shooting star is διᾴττων ἀστήρ, the "darting star" of Anaxagoras and Aristotle, and its little finger ἔσχατος δάκτυλος, "the last finger" (LSJ). From the Coptic Dictionary Online: thunder is ϩⲣⲟⲩⲃⲃⲁⲓ, the "voice of heaven", Monday ⲡⲉⲥⲛⲁⲩ, "the two", and the bat ϭⲓⲛϭⲗⲱ. In Japanese, the grandmother was opoba, "great mother", in the eighth century and oba in the Heian period; the Heian hiccup is さくり, the little finger こゆび, and sugar 砂糖 first appears in a court diary of 1091; the grandmother then became うば in medieval Japanese and ばば in the Edo period, and medieval and Edo rows add the bat かうもり, thunder 雷 and the little finger こゆび (Nihon Kokugo Daijiten). The rows that read Classical Chinese in Cantonese and in Sino-Vietnamese get the same classical words as Middle Chinese: 蝙蝠, 雷, 鯨, 小指, 祖母, 天竺, 中國 and 蜻蛉, read for example biên bức, lôi and kình in Vietnamese. Middle Korean adds the dragonfly ᄌᆞᆫ·자리, attested from 1481, and Middle Persian China 𐭰𐭩𐭭 Čīn; the Goryeo-period word list Gyerim yusa (1103) gives tomorrow as 轄載. Old Norse adds the bat leðrblaka, the "leather-flapper", and the little finger litlifingr (Cleasby–Vigfusson). Phoenician "new", 𐤇𐤃𐤔, is the second half of Qart-ḥadašt, "new city", the Punic name of Carthage, and Ugaritic "yesterday", 𐎛𐎚𐎎𐎍, is the same word as Hebrew אֶתְמוֹל. Biblical Hebrew's little finger is קֹטֶן, "the little one", found only in Rehoboam's boast "my little finger is thicker than my father's loins" (1 Kings 12:10), and its green is ירוק, as in the wild ass that "searches for any green thing" (Job 39:8). The Aramaic bat is עֲטַלֵּפָא, Targum Onkelos's word for the bat of Leviticus 11:19. The Middle Chinese octopus is 章舉, one of the strange sea foods Han Yu met in exile at Chaozhou in 819: "it has eight legs … also called 章魚", says an old commentary.
2026-11-02 — More words in the old languages, continued
  • Word Map: 26 more words in historical languages. Middle Korean adds the whale 고래, the rainbow ·므지·게, the "water door", the thunder ·울에, from a verb "to roar", and the bat :ᄇᆞᆰ·쥐, whose second half is 쥐 "rat". The Old Japanese thunder いかづち is the "awesome spirit", the Syriac Milky Way is the "path of chaff", as in Arabic and Turkish, and Gothic's only word for tomorrow, found once in Matthew 6:30, is related to English yester-. Classical Arabic adds the bat خفاش, the "day-blind one", the little finger خنصر, the grandmother جدة, and شهاب, the Quran's blazing flame, for the shooting star, along with sugar سكر and rice أرز. Old English names Greece Grēcland, "the Greeks' land", Middle English has Grece and Inde, and Old French adds thunder tuneire and blue bleu. Monday is the moon's day in Old High German mānotag, Middle High German māntac and Old Irish lúan, but in Ottoman Turkish it is بازار ایرتسی, "the day after market day". Old East Slavic Китаи, the ancestor of Russian Китай, is named after the Khitan, and Old and Middle English call Germany Germania and Germanie.
