Months: 2026-09 · 2026-08 · 2026-07 · 2026-06 · 2026-05 · 2026-04
2026-09-04 — The Han Map's new dates were printing through their neighbours 🩹
  • The period line added earlier today was not counted when the map works out how much room each label needs, so an ancient reading was one line taller than the map believed and its red date landed on top of the label below. Reported by the owner: 宋明文語's 10–17c printed straight through 元代官話's reading.
  • Fixed, and it cleared three other collisions at the same time. Rows carrying a date also now keep their full space rather than the small overlap the map normally allows between labels — the date is the bottom line, so any slack there is spent printing over whatever sits underneath.
2026-09-04 — Two missing pieces of Cantonese, and a check that was crying wolf 🐺
  • The Cantonese Bai article was missing its whole section on the 1958 Latin alphabet scheme, and the Cantonese Phags-pa article had lost the date of the dissertation it cites. Both written back in.
  • Two other reported gaps turned out to be the checker's fault, not the text's. It looked for years as four digits in a row, which cannot see 「2,000 ปีก่อนคริสตกาล」 in Thai or 「約1,200km」 in Japanese — so it accused both of dropping figures they state plainly. Fixed.
  • With that fixed, the check stopped finding dropped facts and started finding something else: 235 Han Map articles are much shorter in translation than in English — some under a tenth the length. They are summaries that were never brought up to date as the English grew. That is a real backlog and it is now counted honestly rather than reported one date at a time.
2026-09-04 — Two maps were downloading 14 MB of borders on every visit 🗺️
  • The Han Map and the Name Map were fetching the world's country outlines from GitHub at full resolution — 14.6 MB — every time someone opened them. The Word Map stopped doing that months ago and uses a simplified copy served from our own site, 1.9 MB. The other pages were simply never switched over. They are now.
  • That is the difference between about a minute and a half on a slow mobile connection and a couple of seconds, and the page no longer freezes while it unpacks the file. The poster tool and the "my languages" card were on the same old path and are fixed too.
  • Seven more writing systems now come with a font instead of hoping your device has one: Syriac, Cherokee, Thaana (Dhivehi), Canadian Aboriginal syllabics (Inuktitut and Cree), Meetei Mayek, Lepcha and Syloti Nagri — 636 words that showed as empty boxes on any computer without those fonts installed, which is most of them.
  • The Han Map's ancient readings now show their period all the time, not only in the historical view.
2026-09-04 — Ctrl-click a language to put it in the comparison 🖱️
  • On a computer, hold Ctrl (⌘ on a Mac) and click a language on the map to add it to the comparison — click it again the same way to take it out. Requested by the owner. Building a comparison used to mean opening each language's panel and pressing the button inside it; now it is one click per language, straight on the map.
  • A plain click still opens the language panel exactly as before, and tapping on a phone or tablet is untouched.
  • Works on both the Word Map and the Han Map, on the flat map and the globe. The tip is written on the panel's compare button in all 19 interface languages, since a shortcut nobody is told about is a shortcut nobody uses.
2026-09-04 — The Han Map's ancient readings now say when they are from 🏺
  • Tang Chinese, Middle Korean, Tangut, Khitan, Jurchen and the rest now carry their period under the name, the way the Word Map has always shown it — a small dim-red line reading 7–10c, 10–16c, 11–13c. Requested by the owner. It appears in historical mode only; a date under a living reading would read as a mistake.
  • 19 rows dated. Fifteen of them reuse the date the Word Map already gives the same language, so the two maps cannot drift apart; the other four are dated from what the row's own description already said — the 1324 rime book behind Early Mandarin, the 1269–1370 Phags-pa script, the Nara-period texts behind Old Japanese, the Han occupation behind Old Sino-Vietnamese.
  • Proto-Tibeto-Burman is left undated on purpose. Nothing in the atlas gives it a date, and inventing one would be worse than leaving the line off.
2026-09-03 — Sanskrit was quietly in the wrong typeface, and so were 21 other scripts अ
  • The Word Map is set in a serif, and about 8,200 of its cells were not. Reported by the owner, who noticed Sanskrit looking sans-serif next to serif English. It was not imagination: the font list named a serif for Latin, Chinese, Japanese, Korean and Tibetan and for a long tail of ancient scripts, but nothing at all for Devanagari, Arabic, Hebrew, Thai, Bengali, Burmese, Ethiopic, Georgian, Armenian, Khmer, Lao or the South Indian scripts.
  • Those labels were falling through to whatever the reader's device supplies, which on most systems is a sans face. Measured in a browser: the word for honey in Devanagari came out at exactly the width of the sans font, not the serif one. Arabic was the largest group affected at 2,741 cells, then Devanagari at 1,727.
  • Nineteen serif faces added, so the map now reads as one design in every script it can. The fonts are only downloaded when a reader actually looks at those languages.
  • Two scripts still depend on the device: Syriac and Cherokee have no serif available to us.
2026-09-03 — Proto-Tocharian joins the map, and the honey trail finally makes sense 🍯
  • English mead and Chinese 蜜 are usually said to be relatives, and the map could not show why. The chain runs Proto-Indo-European *médʰu → Proto-Tocharian → Old Chinese *mit, but only the two attested Tocharian languages were on the map — and their manuscripts are 5th-10th century CE, centuries later than Old Chinese. A reader checking the claim would find the dates impossible and conclude the map was wrong. It was not wrong, it was missing a step.
  • Proto-Tocharian is now its own row, with its word for honey, *ḿətə. Switch to historical mode and the whole chain is visible at once with its dates: Proto-Tocharian 10th-1st c. BCE beside Old Chinese 13th c. BCE-2nd c. CE, with Tocharian A and B sitting below them as the surviving witnesses, a thousand years later.
  • It sits north of both its daughters, not on top of them. Tocharian A is at Karashahr and Tocharian B at Kucha; putting their ancestor on either oasis would say "same place, same time", which is the misreading the row exists to fix. Tocharian speakers are thought to have come into the Tarim from the north.
  • "Four" now runs the whole way down that branch too — Proto-Tocharian *ćätwerä above, Tocharian A śtwar and Tocharian B śtwer below it. Both Tocharian cells were simply missing before.
  • 17 words are filled, from published reconstructions only — water, mother, daughter, moon, blood, tongue, tooth, heart, three, four, five, hundred, dog, white, earth, wind and honey. The rest are marked unattested rather than guessed at: Proto-Tocharian is rebuilt from two languages and nothing else, so much less of it is settled than for Proto-Indo-European.
2026-09-02 — Simple display is now part of the link 🔗
  • The button that hides the controls for a clean view is now remembered in the URL, on the Word Map, the Han Map and the Name Map. Send someone #simple=1 and they see the map the way you did — no panels, no selectors, just the map.
  • The link wins over your own saved setting, and it applies before the page draws, so there is no flash of the full interface first. If a link says nothing about it, your own setting still applies as before.
  • A link copied while in simple display now carries the setting even if you never touched the URL.
2026-09-01 — The article pages now show you the thing instead of linking to it 🗺️
  • The reading pages carry little instructions — "compare Taa, Khoekhoe, Xhosa and Zulu", "zoom to the Kalahari" — and until now they were only links. You had to leave the article, load the map, and come back. Now the page just answers them where they stand: a set of languages becomes an actual comparison table, and a place becomes an actual map.
  • The comparison tables use the same facts the language pages do — family, speaker numbers, and the word forms with their pronunciation — so the click-language article now shows you ǃqhàa, ǁgam-i and amanzi side by side rather than telling you to go and look. Where the article names a word, that word is the first column.
  • The maps are drawn from the site's own country outlines, at build time. Fifty-seven locations cover every article. There is no script on the page, nothing is fetched from anywhere, and the languages the article is about are pinned on the map with their names in your language — Pirahã shows up as ピダハン語 on the Japanese page and as пирахан on the Russian one.
  • Both work with no JavaScript and are in the page source, so they are readable by anyone the interactive map does not reach — and a search engine sees the table too.
