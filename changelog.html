2026-08-10 — Fact-checking the 70 trivia articles: 411 corrections, and some invented sources
  • The 📚 Trivia articles on the Word Map and Han Map had never been fact-checked. All 70 were put through a review with independent adversarial verification. 411 findings confirmed, and every single article had at least one. The 40 Han Map pieces were generated in one pass and had only ever had their buttons touched, which is most of the explanation.
  • Some of the sources did not exist. A citation to “National Language Research Institute Report 117, Nomura 1997” turned out to name a report that is a different book, from a different year, by an author with no volume in that series at all. Others had the right work under the wrong author, year or publisher — A History of the Korean Language credited to one scholar instead of two, a Bai dissertation moved to the wrong university and dated thirteen years late, a journal volume cited nine years before the journal existed. Three were real books cited for topics they have nothing to do with, including the Comparative Bantu Online Dictionary listed as a source on Sino-Tibetan cognates, with an invented justification attached.
  • Every link was opened and checked. A Glottolog code for Vai returned 404. A WALS link labelled “Rotokas” pointed at Rotuman, a completely different language in Fiji. A feature link for “M in First Person Singular” pointed at the feature for N-M pronouns. A romanisation reference credited to 吳語學堂 linked to 吳語協會, a different organisation with a different scheme.
  • Some of the linguistics was backwards. The article on Japanese readings said 神道 uses the go-on and 道路 the kan-on — the reverse of the truth, in an article that elsewhere states the rule correctly. The Cantonese article claimed the language preserves Middle Chinese voiced initials; it lost them completely, and the tone split is what that loss left behind. The Min Nan showcase example rested on nn̄g as a colloquial reading of 二, but nn̄g belongs to 兩. And the Old Chinese article gave a reconstruction for 山 that contradicted the map cell its own button links to.
  • The Edo-style problem again, elsewhere. Tangut writing was said to vanish in 1227; it was still being carved in 1502. Mongolia's Latin alphabet was said to be reversed in 1946; it was reversed two months after adoption, in March 1941. The Yi Unicode block was given an ending 55 characters too far along. Chunhyangjeon moved to the right century, and the Etruscan linen book back from Roman Egypt to Ptolemaic.
  • 78 map buttons did nothing when clicked. One promised to show 元 while passing 行, and neither is among the 61 characters on the map. Another pointed at a language layer that does not exist — and printed its internal code in the visible label. Since every article exists in 19 languages, four broken attributes meant 78 dead buttons.
  • Two corruptions nobody could have read. In the Thai article on Hakka, the word for “topolect” had been half-overwritten with Cyrillic letters. In the Hindi article on Sino-Vietnamese, an adverb had been replaced by a Korean word. Both shipped with the articles and are invisible to anyone reading in English or Japanese.
  • All of it now reaches readers in every one of the 19 interface languages, not just English. Two new automated checks were added so these classes cannot come back: one verifies every map button points at something real, the other flags any word that mixes two writing systems.
2026-08-09 — The Edo clock ran backwards, and Heian Japanese was 400 years late (reader report)
  • A reader wrote in that the Japanese dialects on the word-order map were sloppy — “no more than LLM translations”. They were right, and the interesting part is where they were right. 99 cells repaired across eight varieties.
  • The living dialects held up. Osaka ends at 2 defective cells out of 100, Hiroshima at 3. Most of what was flagged against them turned out to be a reviewer applying Tokyo norms to a regional variety: うち really is the ordinary Kyoto first person and not only female speech, ちょうだい really is Osaka, どさ really is Tsugaru. Those findings were thrown out.
  • The historical stages did not. Heian Japanese had 21 bad cells — more than the other five varieties combined — because a language stage that ended around 1200 has no room to absorb a doubtful form. 食さ- comes from a 17th-century conjugation shift. もそっと first appears in the 日葡辞書 of 1603. 経験 as a count noun is a Meiji translation word. And a question with an interrogative word needs か with a 連体形 ending, not the yes/no particle や.
  • The clearest single artefact was the Edo clock. Edo hour names count downwards — 九つ, 八つ, 七つ, 六つ, 五つ, 四つ — and they had been read as if they were modern clock numbers. So 7 a.m. was written 七つ時, which is about 4 in the morning; 8 p.m. was 八つ時, which is 2 o’clock; and 3 p.m. was 三つ時, which is not an hour in the system at all. 9 p.m. was 酉の刻, two hours early.
  • Kyoto’s どす had been swapped in for です without inflecting the verb. どす is a contraction of で + おす, so it takes nouns and na-adjectives only — a polite adjective needs おす or the nominaliser ん. The file already knew this (おいしおすなぁ, 悲しいんどすえ), but eleven cells attached どす straight onto an adjective, and ten of them sat in exactly the ten slots where standard Japanese has 〜たいです.
  • One Heian sentence still said “Wi-Fi”. It was the only cell in all 100 containing Latin script, in a row that calques everything else — coffee as 黒き湯, film as 影絵, telephone as 遠声, smartphone as 遠声の器, AI as 作り知恵.
  • The word order itself was sound, which is worth saying. 88–90 of 100 sequences match Tokyo Japanese, and the reviewer assigned to syntax checked them one by one and asked that this be recorded: Japonic word order really is that conservative. Exactly one genuine ordering defect exists.
  • Okinawan got 31 repairs. The verbs were the worst of it — 買った, 話せる, 飲んだ, 開く, 運動しよう and 頑張っとー were simply untranslated Japanese, and “learn” was written 習って+おる in a row that already had the Okinawan ならとーん elsewhere. One sentence had the causal “because” rendered with the conditional, so “she is sad because her friend moved” read “if her friend moves, she is sad”. And “my” was built as 私の with the の respelled, four times over, where Okinawan 我ん has its own genitive わー.
  • Miyako and Yaeyama are not fixed here. Those two rows are 81% character-identical to each other, which is impossible for two languages that are not mutually intelligible, and both are built on Okinawan vocabulary. They need rebuilding rather than patching.
2026-08-07 — Old Korean “mother” and “father” were on invented spellings (reader report)
  • Thanks to @Gutascii on X, who noticed that Silla Old Korean “mother” read 阿米 *əmi while Proto-Ryukyuan is *amma — 阿 is /a/, so the character and the reconstruction were saying different things. 阿米 and 阿比 turned out not to be attestations at all: neither appears in any Silla material. They look like characters supplied to fill the slot.
  • The real form is in the Anminga (安民歌, 756), one of the dated hyangga: 君隱父也 臣隱愛賜尸母史也 — “the lord is father, the minister is a loving mother”. 母史 is the logogram 母 plus 史 as a final-sound phonogram, the same 末音添記 pattern this row already uses for 月羅理 “moon” and 二肹 “two”. Itō Hideto’s 2012 paper in the Tokyo University of Foreign Studies Bulletin glosses the line word by word and reads it əzi.
  • The word is broader than the cell. Itō glosses əzi as 親 “parent”, which is what it becomes in Middle Korean (어시). The “mother” reading here rests on the line’s own contrast — 君隱父也 against 臣隱…母史也 — and on the scribe’s choice of 母 as the semantic character. It is a metaphor in the poem: the minister is like a loving parent to the people.
  • Which makes it a different word from the one that was there. Old Korean 어시 survives into Middle Korean as 어시 “parent”; 어미 “mother” is first attested two and a half centuries later, as 丫彌 in the Gyerim yusa (鷄林類事, 1103). That one now sits on the Goryeo Korean row, where every other cell is already a Gyerim yusa transcription (沒 water, 孛 fire, 契 sun, 妲 moon…), filling two of its gaps.
  • The reconstructed vowels had been right for a reason nobody had written down: Middle Korean 어미 émì has ㅓ and 아비 àpí has ㅏ, so mother and father genuinely differ there.
  • One caution on the Ryukyuan side: Proto-Ryukyuan *amma is not evidence about Korean. Koreanic and Japonic relatedness is unproven, and amma / ama / emi nursery words for “mother” recur across unrelated families the world over. The question was still the right one to ask — it is what turned up the invented characters.
2026-08-07 — 20 new languages, and 1,151 in total
  • Twenty additions, chosen for the gaps they fill rather than the count: Fiji Hindi (the koiné the indentured girmitiyas built in the cane fields, where “cat” is pusi from English), Standard Moroccan Tamazight in Tifinagh, Gheg Albanian, Betawi whose everyday pronouns are Hokkien loans (guè “I” from 我), Latgalian, West Flemish, Kven and Meänkieli, Domari, Nobiin, Fur, Lunda, Tshangla, Lepcha, East Futunan, Ticuna, Wiradjuri, Djambarrpuyngu, and the constructed Interlingue and Ido. Each comes with a full write-up in all 19 interface languages.
  • Several arrive with blanks, and that is deliberate. Domari and Fur have 22 of the 31 concepts, Nobiin and Wiradjuri 23. The authors were told to leave a cell empty rather than produce a plausible-looking form for a language whose documentation does not reach that far. A map with honest gaps is worth more than one that is uniformly filled and quietly wrong.
  • One candidate was rejected: Tosk Albanian turned out to share 30 of 31 forms with the Albanian row already on the map, because Standard Albanian was built on Tosk in 1972. Gheg, at 20 of 31, shows what the split actually looks like — nasal vowels written with a circumflex (hânë “moon”), a real infinitive (me ngranë “to eat”), and shpi where the standard has shtëpi.
2026-08-06 — Konso was Oromo, and Comanche said “how”
  • A review pass over the whole Word Map turned up something worse than a wrong transcription: 19 of the 25 Konso cells were Oromo. The two are related Cushitic languages of Ethiopia, which is how it went unnoticed — abbaa “father”, mana “house”, urjii “star” and akkam “hello” are all perfectly good words, just not Konso ones. The row had cited Ongaye’s A Grammar of Konso as its source the entire time.
  • What gave it away was phonology, not vocabulary. Konso has no voiced stops and no ejectives — it has the rare implosive series ɓ ɗ ʄ ʛ instead, including a uvular implosive almost no other language has. Yet the pronunciation column was full of /b/, /d/, /ɡ/, /dʒ/ and /kʼ/. Six cells could be flagged as impossible without knowing a word of either language. Konso now reads tika (house), kuta (dog), apitta (fire), ʛoyra (tree), aayyaa (mother), sataʔta (heart) — each cited to a page of the grammar.
  • Comanche had two cells holding the same word: “good” and “moon” were both puha, which actually means “medicine, spiritual power”. Fixed to tsaatʉ and mʉa. Worse, “hello” was haʊ — the English-language “how” stereotype of Plains Indian speech, not a Comanche word at all. The real greeting is marʉawe, and “thank you” (ʉra), which had been blank, is now filled. Also corrected: dog (sarii), house (kahni), tree (huupi), mother (pia), father (ahpʉ).
  • Where no source could be found, the cell now says so rather than guessing. Comanche “heart” is blank; “cat” and “love” are kept but flagged as unverified; Konso “moon” uses the word for “month” and is marked as an inference, because the grammar has no entry for “moon” anywhere in its 900 pages.
  • Hopi “tree” was this row’s own word for “one” with the glottal stop dropped (suukya’ → suukya). Now hotski.
2026-08-06 — Five languages were on the map twice
  • Somali, Iñupiaq, Buryat, Onondaga and Khmu each had two rows at the same coordinates, so one pin was drawn directly on top of the other and the duplicate was invisible. Consolidated: 1,138 → 1,135 languages. No word was lost — every cell the removed row filled, the surviving row already filled.
  • Two of them were a different problem from a plain duplicate. The Onondaga row used the code onn, which in ISO 639-3 is Onobasulu, a language of Papua New Guinea; and the Khmu row used xkk, which is Kachok, a Bahnaric language of Cambodia. Both are now on their correct codes (ono and kjg) and keep their translated names in all 19 interface languages.
  • “Cree (Plains)” is renamed simply Cree. It is the macrolanguage, and Plains Cree is a separate row hanging off it — the old name made the parent look like a rival for the child’s slot.
2026-08-06 — “Official in” now says what is actually true
  • For 124 languages the “Official in” field held nothing but a country name. For Japanese or Thai that is correct. For Berom, Tiv and Efik it said “Nigeria”, which reads as though they are official languages of Nigeria — they are not; English is. Same for Konso and Gedeo (“Ethiopia”), and for Northern Qiang, Azhe, Hani, Xibe and Jiamao (“China”).
  • 79 rows rewritten. Where a country grants a real but partial status, the field now says which: Mexico’s sixteen indigenous rows cite the 2003 law that makes them national languages with the same validity as Spanish in their own territory; Guatemala’s five cite the Ley de Idiomas Nacionales; Colombia and Venezuela cite the constitutional articles making indigenous languages official within their own territories. Chipewyan keeps “Canada” — Dëne Sųłiné really is one of the 11 official languages of the Northwest Territories. Nheengatu is co-official in one Amazonian municipality and now says so.
  • Australia’s four languages now state the fact that surprises people: Australia has no official language at federal level. Swiss German no longer claims to be official in Switzerland (the official written language is Standard German). And Ancient Egyptian no longer says “Official in: Ancient Egypt”.
  • Separately, 92 metadata strings turned out to be cut off mid-sentence, some since the rows were first added — Arapaho’s official status ended “…operates a tribal college and”, and Kiowa’s country field was literally “USA (Oklahoma”. All repaired, and a new automated check now blocks any metadata string with an unclosed bracket from being committed.
2026-08-06 — Language pages now speak your language
  • On a language’s own page, the labels were translated but the values were not — a Japanese reader saw 語族 next to “Algonquian”, 文字 next to “Latin”. The interactive map had been translating these all along; the standalone pages had no equivalent. They do now, covering family, speaker counts, countries, official status and script.
  • The vitality field was showing its raw internal value (critically-endangered) instead of a readable one, in every language including English. Now 極めて深刻な危機 / en peligro crítico / на грани вымирания, and the 567 languages marked “safe”, which had no label at all, have one.
2026-08-06 — Han Map: 口 was “mouth”, 立 was “stand”
  • Five Han Map cells held a different character’s reading. Dungan — the Sinitic language of Central Asia written in Cyrillic — gave 口 as зуйъ, which is 嘴 “mouth”, and 立 as җань, which is 站 “to stand”: in both cases the everyday word for the concept instead of the character’s own reading. The Cyrillic for 行 was 走’s, copied one row over.
  • Pu-Xian Min 三 was wrong three times at once. 三 is a 心母 character, which in Pu-Xian gives the language’s celebrated /ɬ/ — the same initial its own 四, 山, 心 and 西 already had; the nasal coda was missing; and the tone was the wrong one. Meixian Hakka 坐 was on the 上聲 tone, missing the classic Hakka shift of 全濁上 to 陰平 that the other four Hakka varieties all show.
  • Middle Korean 食 used ᅀ, the letter Dongguk Jeongun reserves for the 日母 initial — while 十, the same kind of syllable, was already correct with ㅆ. And the Korean native-reading row for 北 showed 북, which is the Sino-Korean reading, next to its own native gloss 된 (as in 된바람, the north wind).
2026-08-04 — Miyako and Yaeyama “eat”/“drink” corrected (reader feedback)
  • Thanks to @Soda_Limer on X, who pointed out that the Japonic varieties stay clearly distinguishable in their plain dictionary form. Following that up turned out to expose a real error: Yaeyama had been classified as Northern Ryukyuan and given Okinawan’s forms verbatim, which is why Okinawan and Yaeyama were showing identical cells. Yaeyama is in fact Southern Ryukyuan, most closely related to Miyako. Both southern rows are now on sourced forms: Miyako “eat” is foː (食ー) and Yaeyama “eat” is ɸoːŋ (食ーん) — f/h-initial reflexes of 食(く)ふ, quite unlike Okinawan’s 噛む-based word. Miyako “drink” is back to numu. Yaeyama “drink” is still an Okinawan copy and is now flagged as provisional until a dictionary source can be checked.
  • The connective-form rule for the mainland dialects is unchanged. It earns its keep on “drink”, where the plain form is 飲む in all seven mainland varieties — no contrast at all — while the connective splits them into 飲んで and Kyushu’s 飲うで.
  • “Good” is now sourced for all three Ryukyuan languages through 琉球大学’s 大琉球語辞典, which cross-searches seven Ryukyuan dictionaries at once. All three had been showing まし maɕi — a noun meaning “preferable”, not the adjective. They are now Okinawan ゆたさん jutasaɴ, Miyako ぞーはン dzoːhaɴ (伊良部方言辞典) and Yaeyama カイシャーン kaiʃaːɴ (石垣方言辞典).
  • A third round, this time with the reader identifying themselves as a speaker of these languages rather than as someone quoting a dictionary. Eleven more Miyako and Yaeyama cells were corrected from that attestation: “mother” (あんな anna / あっぱ appa), “father” (あっちゃ attʃa), “love” (思い゚ umuɨ / 情き nasaki), “cat” (maju / maja), “one”, Miyako “hello” (んみゃーち is a welcome formula, not an everyday greeting), and a dog’s bark わうわう wauwau added for all three Ryukyuan languages — a form 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 independently confirms for Okinawan. Several of these had been holding Okinawan-shaped values in both southern rows, the same copying pattern behind the earlier errors.
  • A second round from the same reader turned up a clear error in Okinawan “love”: it had been 愛 eː, but 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 lists ?ee as an interjection (“hey”), as “among/of”, and as 藍 — indigo. It is not the word for love. That is now 情き nasaki, the form the reporter uses — the dictionary lists it under 情/なさけ and gives “愛のしるし” as a sense. Miyako and Yaeyama “eat”/“drink” also moved to 食い fai/ɸai and 飲み numi, matching the 食(く)ふ stem their conjugation tables give, and the central vowel ɨ was restored in Yaeyama “fire” (pɨː) and both southern rows’ “heart” (kɨmu).
  • Following that up, Okinawan was re-checked against 国立国語研究所’s 沖縄語辞典 (Shuri). “Good” was wrong: it had been まし maɕi, but the dictionary lists masi as a noun meaning “better, preferable”, not the adjective. Okinawan for “good” is ゆたしゃん jutaɕaɴ. The verbs were right on the map (食でぃ kadi, 飲でぃ nudi) but recorded under the wrong dictionary forms — they are かぬん kanuN and ぬぬん nunuN, and the dictionary’s own conjugation codes (=maN, =di) confirm the connective.
2026-08-04 — New language: Öömrang (Amrum North Frisian)
  • Added Öömrang, the North Frisian dialect of the island of Amrum off the German North Sea coast — one of only a few hundred remaining speakers. It’s a distinct insular variety, sharply different from mainland North Frisian: “fire” is ial (not iilj), “two” is tau (not twäär), “water” weeder, “father” aatj, “I” ik. Every form was sourced and cross-checked against Wiktionary (Föhr-Amrum), the Öömrang Wikipedia, and dialect grammars. Thanks to @avzaagzonunaada for the suggestion.
2026-08-04 — New language: Taiwan Mandarin (臺灣華語)
  • Added Taiwan Mandarin (國語 / 臺灣華語) at Taipei — Taiwan’s standard Mandarin, written in Traditional characters across all the core words (太陽, 媽媽, 愛, 樹, 貓, 謝謝…), distinct from the Simplified forms of Mainland Mandarin. Its most charming difference: the “@” sign is 小老鼠 — “little mouse” — where the Mainland says 艾特. Comes with a full write-up in all 19 interface languages.
2026-08-04 — The “@” sign: what each name means, and 100+ languages
  • The “@” map grew to over 100 languages — now including Cantonese, the national English standards (US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, India, Singapore…), every Spanish variety (all say arroba), and Quebec French, which calls it a commercial rather than France’s arobase.
  • New for the “@” word only: each name now shows what it literally means, as a little emoji gloss right on the map — 🐌 snail, 🐕 dog, 🐒 monkey’s tail, 🦆 duckling, 🐘 elephant’s-trunk ‘a’, ⚖️ arroba (an old unit of weight) — translated into all 20 interface languages.
2026-08-03 — New word: the “@” sign 🧪
  • Added the spoken name of the “@” symbol as a word concept — one of the most colourful things to map. It’s a snail in Italian (chiocciola) and Welsh (malwen), a monkey’s tail in Dutch (apenstaartje), a little monkey in Serbian and Bulgarian, a dog in Russian (собака), a duckling in Greek (παπάκι), a strudel in Hebrew (שטרודל), rollmops in Czech/Slovak (zavináč), an elephant’s trunk in Swedish/Danish (snabel-a), a worm in Hungarian (kukac), a whelk in Korean (골뱅이) — and simply “at” borrowed in many others. ~40 languages so far.
2026-08-03 — Okinawan corrections (reader feedback)
  • Thanks to @iru_han2 on X, four Okinawan (うちなーぐち) entries were fixed. Okinawan phonemically distinguishes a glottal stop, so “mother” (あんまー), “you” (やー) and “dog” (犬) now carry it in their transcriptions — ʔammaː, ʔjaː (the pronoun, distinct from やー jaː “house”) and ʔiɴ. And “tree” (木) is kiː, not the earlier tɕiː.
