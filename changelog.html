2026-11-20 — Word Map: more words that should have been there
  • The search for missing words went on through languages whose Bibles and Jehovah's Witnesses publications nobody had read for the map yet. Weekdays came from the dated headings of their daily texts and meeting schedules, and basic words from Bible verses: "left" and "right" from the sheep and goats of Matthew 25, "husband" and "wife" from John 4, "tear" from Revelation 21.
  • Mende, Khasi, Palauan, Chol and Belize Kriol now have their days of the week. Khasi names them "first", "second", "congregation day", "washing day" and "God's day". In East Futuna, Wednesday and Friday are the church's felia fā and felia ono, "fourth" and "sixth".
  • Old gaps filled with basic words: Uspanteko "yesterday", "husband" and "maize"; Niuean "king", "gold" and "tear" (hihina mata, "eye water"); Kabardian "lion", "island" and "camel"; Choctaw "bread", "king" and "island". Nuer "left" and "right" also name the two kingdoms of Israel, Dinka calls the whale "big fish", and Kalenjin uses one word for "language" and "mouth".
  • Older layers too. Middle Japanese "camel" was takuda, not rakuda, and Old Japanese already had pazikami, the "mouth-stinging" pepper of a Kojiki song. A Chinese astrological text of 759 gives the seven-day round 日曜…土曜, now on the Classical Chinese and Japanese kanbun rows. In Sanaa Arabic the butterfly is the "lamp-extinguisher".
  • Siberia and the Caucasus: Karakalpak "king" is patsha, and its word for "wife" is the same as "woman"; Tuvan reads "lion" as арзылаң from its Bible; Chechen calls the octopus "eight-leg". In Sakha the @ sign is ыт, "dog".
  • Over forty African languages had gaps in their basic words. Ga uses one word, naa, for both grandmothers: the Bible gives it to Lois, a mother's mother, and to Maacah, a father's mother. Tigrinya has a separate word for each side's uncle. Moore got "silk", "cotton", "China" and "India" from one sentence about the old trade routes. Kinyarwanda calls the Milky Way Inzira Nyamata, the "milk road", and Northern Sotho thunder is "the rumble of the lightning".
  • Across Indonesia, Taiwan and the Pacific: the Bunun Bible gave "husband", "wife", "tear", "money" and "island", and Bunun "husband" is the same word as "man". The Tahitian bat is ʻiore pererau, a "rat with wings". In Pangasinan, "kiss" and "aroma" are one word, angob. In Gilbertese, wi is both "tooth" and "mouth".
  • South Asia and the Himalayas: a 1915 Ho grammar gave most of the week. In Ho, "week" is hát, the weekly "market", and the rainbow is named after the rulbing, a milk-drinking snake believed to stop the rain. Fiji Hindi now has "rice", "snake", "elephant" and "money", and Bodo has "yesterday" and "tomorrow". In Sylheti, "kiss" is the same word as "to smell".
  • The Americas: an open Bororo dictionary filled twenty cells, Iñupiaq got its thumb, little finger and grandmother from MacLean's dictionary, and Arapaho Saturday is the "rations day". Mohawk "monkey" means "it eats lice", the Ho-Chunk lion is "half the body is furry", and in Creek "iron" and "stone" are one word.
  • Europe's smaller languages: Romansh filled thirty cells from its official dictionary, and Livonian twenty-six. The Livonian Milky Way is the "birds' road" and the Mingrelian one the "shepherd's road". Gagauz had no "left", "gold" or "king" although its New Testament has them all. In Cornish a hangover is kurun spern, a "crown of thorns", and the Aromanian sunflower is the "eye of the sun".
  • On the Word Map and Han Map, the arrow from a selected language to its panel now comes out from behind the middle of the language's box, so no line crosses the box.
  • The North Korean row had copied many South Korean words. Checked against a North Korean dictionary, it now has 빠다 for butter, 안해 for wife, 강냉이 for maize, 목화 for cotton, 락타 for camel, 록색 for green and 이발 for tooth. The rest really are the same in both Koreas. The Hamgyong row now has its own dialect forms from Ogura Shinpei's 1944 dialect maps (세 tongue, 감지 potato, 흘기 earth, 낭기 tree), and Yanbian Korean writes 락타, 안해 and 록색 but keeps 버터 and 옥수수, as its newspapers do.

2026-11-18 — Word Map: words that should have been there
  • After Inner Mongolian turned out to lack "wife", we went looking for the same kind of gap everywhere, one region at a time. Words added to the map after a language's row was made had often never been filled in, even when the row's own dictionary or Bible had them. So far about 2,900 words have been added across Chinese dialects, the minority languages of southwest China and Southeast Asia, the Philippines, Indonesia, Taiwan and northern India: Hokkien and Hakka "wife", "money" and "window"; all seven weekdays for Wa and Surin Khmer, which had none; left and right, husband and wife for dozens of languages, many of which use one word for "spouse".
  • Old languages now sometimes show what people used before a thing existed, with a note saying so: in Koine Greek and Latin a chili is pepper, the hot spice of the day; a turkey is the guinea fowl, whose Greek name μελεαγρίς Linnaeus later gave to the turkey; and a Roman "lol" is the hahahae of Terence's comedies. Real period phrases are there too: Romans answered a sneeze with salvē and raised a cup with bene tē.
  • The search has now covered India and the Himalaya, Siberia and the Caucasus, about 4,700 words so far in all. Many came straight from the very dictionary or Bible a row was first built from: Abaza "bat" is "night bird", Karachay-Balkar names its weekdays after saints, and Gujarati, Marwari and Mundari "right" is the hand you eat with.
  • More old languages show what stood in for things that did not yet exist: paper is a clay tablet in Sumerian and Akkadian, papyrus in Egyptian and Coptic, bamboo slips in Old Chinese and a palm leaf in Pali; soap is natron, plant ash or Tang China's bean washing powder; and the Mongols' window is the smoke-hole in the roof of the felt tent. Old Chinese now has 右 "right" to go with its 左 "left".
  • The search moved on to Iran, Kurdistan and the Caucasus. Several rows came from a word list that held far more than the map used, so Kryts, Tsakhur, Khinalug, Tabasaran and Rutul now have their words for bread, snake, wife and gold. Qashqai gained "head", "horse" and "rain" from the very glossary its row cites, and Sudanese Arabic calls the Milky Way "the ram's drag".
  • Japanese, Korean and Germanic history are filled in too. Middle English finally has "wife", Middle High German "thousand" and "yellow", Old Saxon "left" and "right", and Luther's Bible gives "gel" for yellow. The stand-ins carry on: parchment is the paper of Gothic, Old Norse and Old High German, the computer of Old English and Prakrit is the reckoner who counted, and the sunflower is the flower said to follow the sun.
  • West Africa next: Mampruli and Dagbani have their words for wife, husband, money and the days of the week, and their "left" and "right" also mean north and south. In Igbo and Ewe the right hand is "the eating hand". Hausa-speaking rows still wait for a dictionary that marks vowel length.
  • The old languages of the Americas now say maize, chocolate, chili and tobacco in their own words. Tupi naná is where the word "ananas" for pineapple comes from. Where something had not arrived yet there is a note: the Classic Maya "wine" is pulque, and the "lion" of the Inca, Aztec and Muisca is the puma the Spanish called león. Proto-languages get only reconstructions that scholars have actually published, never stand-ins. That still brought Proto-Japonic "tear", "rainbow" and "whale", and the Proto-Mayan for "twenty", which is "one person".
  • The search went round the rest of the world: East and Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean, Western Europe's regional languages, Central and Eastern Europe and the Baltic and Nordic fringe, the Caribbean and Suriname creoles, Mexico and Central America, and North America's Indigenous languages. That added about 3,000 words in all. Mayan rows now have the vocabulary their dictionaries always had. Cherokee calls the elephant by its word for butterfly, after its flapping ears, and Sm'algyax has one word for both husband and wife. A new cell has to come with its pronunciation printed in the source, or else the row's spelling must show exactly how it sounds. Otherwise it waits.
  • Cheers! is now said in 38 more languages. Maltese and Breton say "health", Hawaiian says ʻōkole maluna, "bottoms up", and in Botswana the toast pula, "rain", is also the country's motto and currency. Telugu, Kannada, Nepali and other South Asian languages raise their glasses with the English word itself. Web text shows them doing it, and Kyrgyz, Tatar and Tajik toast "to your health".
  • Toasts reach back in time too, with 23 historical languages: Greek προπίνω σοι, Egyptian "for your ka" from a tomb banquet, the Kojiki's sake song ending "drink it to the last drop, sasa!", the Shijing's 萬壽無疆, Old Norse heill ver þú, and Rabbi Akiva's "wine and life", an early l'chaim.
  • Another 2,000 or so words came in from South America, Polynesia, Australia and New Guinea, and from rows whose New Testament had them all along. Rossel Island's money is ndapî, its famous shell money. In Bororo the right is "the good side" and the left "the bad side". The Choctaw right hand is "the hand one eats with". Many languages make the tear from "eye" and "water". The Arabic varieties caught up as well: Levantine finally has left, right, snake, wife and the days of the week.

2026-11-17 — Old languages, one era per row
  • Two new rows split off from older ones: Koine Greek, the Greek of the New Testament and the papyri, where a bird is πετεινόν and Sunday is Κυριακή; and Late Latin, the Latin of the Vulgate and Isidore, where a cat is cattus, a horse caballus and fire focus, the words the Romance languages kept.
