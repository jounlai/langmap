The Chinese word 蜜 mì may be a distant relative of English “mead.”

Proto-Indo-European appears to have had at least two words for honey:

*mélit- “honey” — seen in Latin mel, Greek méli, and Hittite melit.

*médʰu “honey; mead” — Sanskrit madhu, Old Church Slavonic medъ, and Old English medu, the source of English mead.

So Sanskrit madhu does not come from *mélit-. It belongs to the other PIE word.

That second word also travelled east. It is reconstructed in Proto-Tocharian as *ḿət(ə), with Tocharian B mit “honey.” Tocharian was an Indo-European branch spoken in the Tarim Basin. Its surviving manuscripts date roughly from the 5th to 10th centuries, so Proto-Tocharian itself is reconstructed rather than directly attested.

The usual hypothesis is that this Tocharian word entered Old Chinese as *mit and was written 蜜. The Chinese–Tocharian comparison goes back to Polivanov in 1916, although the details remain debated.

The word later spread through the Sinitic and classical Chinese sphere:

Mandarin mì
Cantonese mat6
Japanese mitsu
Sino-Vietnamese mật
Sino-Korean mil

Korean still commonly uses its native word 꿀 for honey, but 蜜 survives as mil in compounds.

One ancient word may thus connect honey-words from South Asia to East Asia and Europe—through inheritance, borrowing, and cultural transmission.
