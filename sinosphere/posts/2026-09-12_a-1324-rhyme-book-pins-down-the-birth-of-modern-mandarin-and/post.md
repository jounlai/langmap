A 1324 rhyme book pins down the birth of modern Mandarin, and it was written for playwrights.

In the mid-Yuan, the dramatist and prosodist Zhou Deqing(周德清) compiled the Zhongyuan Yinyun(*中原音韻*). The purpose was practical: librettists working on the hugely popular zaju(雜劇) operas needed to know what actually rhymed in northern speech.

For historical linguistics it turned out to be the decisive document for the shift from Middle Chinese (roughly 600 AD) to early modern Mandarin.

What it proves is that the entering tone had already gone in the north. That fourth tone category of Tang and Song was not a pitch at all — it was the class of syllables ending in -p, -t or -k. By 1324 those endings were gone from northern speech, and the tone system had reorganised into what Mandarin has today.

Not a treatise. A working handbook — which is why it records real sound.
