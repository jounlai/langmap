How far up does a “foot” go?

Languages disagree surprisingly often about where the foot ends and the leg begins.

In some languages, the same basic word covers both FOOT and LEG. Others draw a clear lexical boundary between them. This is a well-known typological pattern: FOOT–LEG is one of the most common body-part colexifications across languages.

Japanese and Korean make a striking contrast.

Japanese 足 *ashi* can refer to both the foot and the leg in ordinary usage. Korean draws a clear line: 발 *bal* “foot” vs. 다리 *dari* “leg”.

But the upper limb shows that languages are not always symmetrical. Korean also distinguishes 손 *son* “hand” from 팔 *pal* “arm”, while Japanese distinguishes 手 *te* “hand” from 腕 *ude* “arm”. Japanese therefore cuts the upper and lower limbs differently.

The Sinitic languages are internally diverse too.

Mandarin distinguishes 腳 *jiǎo* “foot” from 腿 *tuǐ* “leg”. Cantonese 腳 *goek3*, however, can cover both the foot and the leg, and Taiwanese Hokkien 跤 *kha* likewise means both “foot” and “leg”. Shanghainese also allows 腳 for both, while Hakka varieties may use forms such as 腳 for “foot” and 腳髀 for “leg”.

So this is not simply a north–south split within Chinese.

Europe has similar contrasts. Romanian *picior* can mean both “foot” and “leg”, unlike Spanish *pie/pierna*, French *pied/jambe*, or Italian *piede/gamba*. Polish distinguishes *stopa* “foot” from *noga* “leg”, while Czech and Slovak *noha* can cover both.

Some languages even build a more specific “foot” expression from a broader limb word. Chuvash, for example, has *ура* for the leg/foot and *ура лаппи* for the foot; compare *ал лаппи* “palm of the hand”.

One near-universal does survive: languages almost never use the same word for HAND and FOOT themselves. A large cross-linguistic comparison found only a very rare exception.

Same human body, very different lexical boundaries.
