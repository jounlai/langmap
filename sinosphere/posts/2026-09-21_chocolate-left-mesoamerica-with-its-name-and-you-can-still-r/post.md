Chocolate left Mesoamerica with its name, and you can still recognize it almost everywhere.

Spanish chocolate is generally traced to Nahuatl, although the exact Nahuatl form behind it is still debated.

From Spanish, the word spread through Europe and far beyond:

chocolate

chocolat

Schokolade

cioccolato

шоколад

שוקולד

चॉकलेट

The Sinosphere is the detail worth noticing.

Chinese often translates foreign concepts by meaning, but not this time. Mandarin has 巧克力 qiǎokèlì, while Cantonese has 朱古力 zyu1 gu2 lik6. Both reproduce the sound rather than the meaning.

Japanese has チョコレート chokorēto, commonly shortened to チョコ choko.

Korean adds another twist. South Korea writes 초콜릿 chokollit, while North Korean usage commonly has 쵸콜레트 chyokolleteu. The same foreign word ended up with different standard forms on the two sides of Korea.

Vietnam took a different route again.

As part of French Indochina, Vietnam absorbed a large layer of French vocabulary. Chocolate came through French chocolat and became sô-cô-la.

So within the old Chinese-character cultural sphere, the same word arrived through several different channels:

Mandarin 巧克力

Cantonese 朱古力

Japanese チョコレート

South Korean 초콜릿

North Korean 쵸콜레트

Vietnamese sô-cô-la

There does not seem to be an established Chữ Nôm spelling for sô-cô-la. 巧克力 can be read in Sino-Vietnamese as xảo khắc lực, but that is simply the Hán-Việt reading of the Chinese transcription, not the traditional Vietnamese word for chocolate.

Finnish looks unusually short too: suklaa. It came through Swedish choklad and was reshaped on its way into Finnish.

And chocolate is not alone.

Tomato comes from Nahuatl tomatl.

Avocado comes through Spanish aguacate from Nahuatl āhuacatl.

Chocolate is also generally traced back to Nahuatl, though its exact early form remains disputed.

Three words from Mesoamerica that ended up on tables almost everywhere.
