# Review 530 — closed

**Question:** Would any nickname offend, belittle or misrepresent the people who speak that
language — or read as a claim the wearer has no standing to make?

**Scope:** all 32 entries then in `lang_nicknames.js`. **26 cleared, 6 findings, 3 dropped.**

**Not one of the 32 is a slur.** The rule-5 filter has held. Everything found is register,
ownership or political charge — and two entries turned out to be *better* than the formal name they
sit beside. `en.pcm` Naijá was adopted by the Naijá Langwej Akedemi **to escape** a slur ("the term
'pidgin' has helped to encourage derogatory connotations"). And `en.nds` **inverts the expected
direction**: Universität Oldenburg says "the term *Low German* has been used pejoratively for
centuries", and academics reached for it because Plattdeutsch "was probably not refined enough" —
so the nickname is the speakers' word and the **formal name** carries the contempt.

## The finding that generalises: a shirt leaves the browser

Rule 2 says a nickname never crosses UI languages — Japanese gets シングリッシュ, German gets
Singlish, and a UI with no attested nickname gets the formal name. **That firewall is a property of
the browser session. A printed object has no UI.**

`ja.ang` = アングロサクソン語 was kept for Japanese and rejected for English, and the *reasoning* was
right: the dog-whistle is a specifically anglophone-political artefact — the 2021 America First
Caucus "Anglo-Saxon political traditions", the 1922 Anglo-Saxon Clubs of America — with no Japanese
counterpart and no group in Japan it targets. ISSEME's own wording sharpens rather than softens the
English rejection: they did not ban scholarly use ("members are at liberty to use whatever
terminology most accurately describes what they are doing"), they renamed over connotations **"in
public discourse"** — and a T-shirt is public discourse where a footnote is not.

But asked what the shirt says, the answer is "Anglo-Saxon", in English, to an English reader. It
also fails rule 1 outright: 古英語 is the everyday *and* academic Japanese name, is the map's formal
name, and is **shorter** — and the dataset's own source shows the dependence running backwards,
世界大百科事典 defining it as 「古英語(Old English)(略称OE)の別称」. **Dropped**, and the file's rules
gained a sixth: before keeping a per-UI nickname that transliterates a term rejected in another UI,
ask whether it survives leaving the browser.

## Also dropped

- **`en.yi` mame-loshn — ownership, not offence.** No source calls it offensive and that is on the
  record. It fails because it names a **relationship**, not a language: JEL's first sense is "A
  mother tongue" and its own cited author glosses it mid-sentence. Printed alone across a chest
  with no "Yiddish" beside it, it is a first-person claim by whoever is wearing it. Yiddish also
  carries a live in-community critique of mass-producing exactly this register. "Yiddish" is
  already short and warm, so the nickname bought nothing.
- **`en.sco` Lallans — scope.** Lallans is the south/central dialects plus the 20th-century
  literary "synthetic Scots"; **Doric and Insular Scots sit outside it**. A Doric speaker who picks
  "Scots" would be handed a variety that is not theirs. "Scots" is shorter and covers everybody.

## Carried as notes

**`en.ak` Twi** had already gone in round 527 on taxonomy; this round sharpens why it mattered on a
garment — "Fante is always referred to as Fante", and Ghana chose "Akan" precisely so the cover name
would not belong to one group.

**`en.pal` Pahlavi**: bare across a chest, to an Iranian-diaspora reader, it reads first as the
dynasty. `ja.pal` パフラヴィー語 does not have the problem because 語 disambiguates. Recorded only.

**`ja.my`**: ビルマ語 vs ミャンマー語 is not a nickname question, it is a **position**. The Burmese
pro-democracy diaspora in Japan says ビルマ because the renaming was done unilaterally by a
government without popular mandate; the Japanese state changed its usage in step with SLORC; and
ビルマ carries a wartime register besides. Round 527 had made ビルマ語 the formal Japanese name for
consistency with English "Burmese" — **this round reverted that**. It is the owner's call to make
knowingly, not one to inherit from a dictionary headword.

## The repair this round asked for first

`docs/lang-nickname-sources.md` said "'Ebonics' is rejected below" — **and there was no below.**
Every rule-5 rejection lived only in reviewers' memory, for the one rule the guard cannot enforce,
with a dangling cross-reference that made it look as though a record existed. The rejections table
is now part of the file: a rejection that is not written down gets re-proposed.

## One rejection was filed under the wrong ground

**Strine is not a slur.** Australians coined it, publish it, and treat it as heritage. Filing it
beside Gypsy and 支那語 misstated both the word and the Australians who use it. The real objection is
**register**: it is a mock-phonetic respelling of "Australian" in a broad accent — "Emma Chisit" for
"how much is it" — so the name works by imitating the accent it names, self-deprecation from inside
and mimicry from outside. Outcome right, ground wrong; the record now says so.

Conversely **閩南語 held up under scrutiny**: Taiwanese civic groups objected on character grounds
(閩 contains 虫; 南 pairs with 蠻夷) and the MOE renamed its certification 臺灣台語. Adding *no*
nickname rather than substituting 台語 was also right — Hakka groups warn that 台語 implies other
mother tongues are not Taiwanese, and Kinmen and Matsu speakers resist it. **Where every available
nickname belongs to one side, the formal name is the answer.** Taki-Taki is the weakest of the ten
rejections and needs a real citation before it could ever be revisited.
